Step 01 · The question

Understand the claim before checking it

Imagine two preparations of the same system. One begins hotter than the other. Put both in contact with the same cooler bath and let both evolve under the same rules. Usually the hotter preparation starts farther from the bath's equilibrium. The question is whether it can later be closer.

Here “closer” means a specified mathematical distance between probability distributions. It need not mean a lower thermometer reading. The main example has four possible states. A proposed shortcut inspects every group of three states and tries to rule out unusual cooling. The example shows that this shortcut can miss a collective effect.

  1. Verify that the four-state model is a valid thermalizing model.
  2. Apply exactly the stated three-state tests to all four triplets.
  3. Verify that the hotter preparation begins farther away.
  4. Prove that it eventually becomes closer.

A counterexample is one valid case contradicting a universal statement. It does not say that every system behaves that way. This counterexample concerns a finite-temperature conjecture; it does not refute the separate zero-temperature theorem.

Checkpoint: What would not be enough?

A plot with a crossing, without checked inputs and error bounds, would not establish the full claim. Nor would a passing triplet test on only three of the four triplets.

Step 02 · Probability

Think of four boxes holding one unit in total

The numbers p₁, p₂, p₃, p₄ describe how likely the system is to be in each state. Each lies between zero and one, and together they sum to one. A vector is simply an ordered list. We write the probabilities in a column.

p = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)
0.4 + 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 1

These probabilities can also describe the fractions in a very large collection of identical systems. They do not mean that a single classical system is partly in each state.

If a state is assigned a value wᵢ, its probability-weighted average is Σᵢwᵢpᵢ. The symbol Σ means “add the following terms.” For values (1, −1, 0, 0), the average in the example is 0.4 − 0.3 = 0.1. Negative values of w are allowed: w is an observable, not a probability.

Try it: average (2, 0, −1, 3)

2(0.4) + 0(0.3) − 1(0.2) + 3(0.1) = 0.9. We will use exactly this operation to calculate a slow-mode overlap.

Step 03 · The Gibbs distribution

Higher energy is less likely at equilibrium

Assign an energy Eᵢ to each state. At temperature T, give it an unnormalized weight exp(−βEᵢ), where β = 1/(kBT). We choose units with kB = 1 and bath βb = 1. The exponential exp(x) is eˣ; its inverse is the natural logarithm ln.

Z(β) = exp(−βE₁) + exp(−βE₂) + exp(−βE₃) + exp(−βE₄)
πᵢ(β) = exp(−βEᵢ) / Z(β)

Z is just the normalizing total. The Greek letter π here names the equilibrium probability list; it is not the circle constant. A smaller β means a hotter preparation. At β near zero the probabilities are nearly equal. Cooling increases β and favors low-energy states.

It is convenient to store the bath weights qᵢ = exp(−Eᵢ). Then Eᵢ = −ln(qᵢ), and at any preparation temperature the weights are qᵢβ. In this project the bath remains fixed while the preparation changes.

Checkpoint: Which is hotter, β = 0.001 or β = 0.01?

β = 0.001 is hotter. In bath-temperature units their temperatures are 1000 and 100. Both are hotter than the bath at T = 1. These very hot preparations are part of a mathematical counterexample, not the later moderate-range physical model.

Step 04 · Rates

Probability flows between boxes

A transition rate is a probability per unit time, to first order over a very short interval. Rate 2 does not mean probability 2. Over a short time Δt, it gives a transition probability approximately 2Δt. Multiple transitions make this approximation unsuitable for a large Δt.

Mᵢⱼ is the rate from state j to state i. Think “column gives the starting box, row gives the destination.” The diagonal records loss, so it is negative.

Mⱼⱼ = −Σᵢ≠ⱼ Mᵢⱼ
dpᵢ/dt = Σⱼ Mᵢⱼ pⱼ
Each column of M sums to zero.

A matrix is a table of numbers. Multiplying M by p means taking each row's weighted sum. The equation above is a collection of ordinary differential equations. Column sums of zero make the total derivative of p₁ + p₂ + p₃ + p₄ zero. Positive off-diagonal rates ensure that probability cannot flow out of an empty box.

A complete two-box example
M = [ −2   1 ]
    [  2  −1 ]
dp₁/dt = −2p₁ + p₂
dp₂/dt =  2p₁ − p₂

Add the equations to get zero. The equilibrium is (1/3, 2/3): the outgoing flow 2(1/3) equals the returning flow 1(2/3).

Step 05 · Equilibrium

Check every forward-and-backward pair

Detailed balance means that at the bath distribution, each pair has equal flows in both directions:

Mᵢⱼ qⱼ = Mⱼᵢ qᵢ.

Dividing both sides by Q = q₁ + q₂ + q₃ + q₄ gives equal flows for the actual probabilities. There may still be individual transitions at equilibrium; their average flows cancel.

For i < j, let aᵢⱼ be the downward rate from higher-energy j to lower-energy i. To impose detailed balance, define the upward rate as aⱼᵢ = aᵢⱼ qⱼ/qᵢ. Since qⱼ < qᵢ, going up costs energy and is slower.

Example: q₁ = 1, q₂ = 199/200, a₁₂ = 1
a₂₁ = 1 × (199/200)/1 = 199/200
a₁₂ q₂ = 199/200 = a₂₁ q₁.

Check all six pairs and all four column sums. Together these give Mπb = 0: the bath distribution stays fixed.

Step 07 · Four triplets

Apply the original test without changing it

The four ordered triplets are 123, 124, 134, and 234. For each i < j < k, set A = aᵢₖ, B = aⱼₖ, C = aᵢⱼ, t = qⱼ/qᵢ, and u = qₖ/qⱼ. The letters t and u in this test are weight ratios, not time.

κ = (A+B)/[C(1+t)]
G = At − B
F = A(1+tu/2) − B(1+u/2) − C(1−t)
P = (κ ≥ 1 AND F > 0) OR (κ ≤ 1 AND G > 0)

“P is false” means that this necessary-condition test fails. It is not a claim that every passing triplet necessarily has an effect. Notice that κ comparisons include equality, whereas the F and G comparisons do not.

Step 08 · Logic and algebra

Check the useful shortcut in all three cases

The test fails exactly when both F ≤ 0 and G ≤ 0. This is not true of arbitrary pairs of numbers with arbitrary branches; it follows from a special identity.

H = A+B−C(1+t), d = (1−t)/(1+t), h = 2/(1+t)+u/2
F = hG + dH, with h > 0 and d > 0.
  1. H > 0: κ > 1, so failure means F ≤ 0. Rearranging gives hG = F−dH < 0. Thus G < 0.
  2. H < 0: κ < 1, so failure means G ≤ 0. Then F = hG+dH < 0.
  3. H = 0: κ = 1. Both branches apply, and F = hG. Failure means neither strict sign is positive, so F and G are both nonpositive.

Conversely, if both are nonpositive, neither branch can pass. At fixed q, each F and G is linear in the downward rates, so the allowed rates form an intersection of half-spaces. A half-space is simply one side of a flat boundary. Equalities belong to the allowed set.

Checkpoint: If κ = 1 and F = G = 0, does the test pass?

No. The sign tests require strict positivity. This matters in the limiting family used for the 25% theorem.

Step 09 · Exponential decay

A mode is a pattern that keeps its shape

First recall the calculus equation y′ = λy. Its solution is y(t) = y(0)eλt. If λ is negative, the pattern decays. If λ = −2, it decays more slowly than λ = −10.

For several boxes, a right eigenvector v is a pattern satisfying Mv = λv. Starting with that pattern's deviation from equilibrium, the deviation remains proportional to v and its size changes by eλt. A left eigenvector w satisfies wᵀM = λwᵀ. It measures how much of that mode is present.

w = (1, −759/1000, −48957/80000, −1/250)
λ₂ = −4966671/250000 = −19.866684
Σ qᵢwᵢ = 0

For this model, multiplying out all four columns verifies the left equation exactly. A matching right vector is vᵢ = qᵢwᵢ/N, where N = Σqᵢwᵢ². This makes Σwᵢvᵢ = 1. Choosing a scale for w changes the numerical overlap but not the hot/cold amplitude ratio.

Why does a left eigenvector measure an independently decaying component?

Differentiate its average along a trajectory: d(wᵀp)/dt = wᵀp′ = wᵀMp = λwᵀp. The first equality uses that w is fixed while the system evolves. This is the scalar equation y′ = λy again, so wᵀp(t) = eλtwᵀp(0). Also wᵀMπb = 0 = λwᵀπb; since λ is nonzero, its equilibrium average is zero. This explains both the exponential behavior and why b can be calculated directly from the initial probabilities.

Make sure it is the slow mode

Being an eigenvector is not enough. There are three decaying modes. The characteristic polynomial, whose roots are eigenvalues, factors as x(x−λ₂) times a quadratic. Exact opposite signs at −337 and −336 locate one quadratic root; opposite signs at −60 and −59 locate the other. A quadratic has only two roots, so they are accounted for. Both decay faster than λ₂, which lies between −20 and −19.

Why do opposite signs locate a root?

A polynomial is continuous. To go from negative to positive it must pass through zero. The intervals are disjoint, so these are distinct roots. The new checker reconstructs all polynomial coefficients from determinants rather than trusting decimal eigenvalues.

Step 11 · Distance reversal

Connect a smaller overlap to actual relaxation

Define a nonnegative distance-like quantity by squaring each probability error, dividing by qᵢ, and adding:

Dq(p) = Σ (pᵢ−πb,i)²/qᵢ.

It is zero only at equilibrium. The exact initial values satisfy Dq(cold) < 24 < Dq(hot). Thus the hotter preparation really starts farther away according to this measure.

At long times, the faster patterns disappear relative to the slowest one. In more precise notation, p(t)−πb = b(β)v eλ₂t + faster-decaying terms. Insert this into the squared distance. The leading factor shared by both preparations cancels in their ratio:

Dq(hot, t) / Dq(cold, t) → [b(hot)/b(cold)]² < 1.

A limit strictly below one means that after some finite time the ratio stays below one. Initially it is above one. Continuous evolution therefore has at least one crossing in between. This establishes existence, not the earliest crossing time or uniqueness of the crossing.

Formal support: The Lean development proves the evolution equation, uniqueness of its solution, the limiting ordering, and the initial ordering for this rational witness. Its distance is Dq. It does not certify a numerical crossing time or the KL argument below.
What happens to KL distance?

Relative entropy, or KL divergence, is DKL(p‖π) = Σpᵢ ln(pᵢ/πᵢ). Near equilibrium, the Taylor expansion of ln gives DKL ≈ ½Σ(pᵢ−πᵢ)²/πᵢ. Because πᵢ = qᵢ/Q, this is QDq/2. Here Var(E) = Σπᵢ(Eᵢ−U)² is the variance, with mean energy U = ΣπᵢEᵢ. It is positive when distinct energy levels all have positive probability. The same strict late-time ratio follows analytically. For Gibbs preparations, differentiation gives dDKL/dβ = (β−1)Var(E), negative on the cooling interval 0 < β < 1. So the hotter one also starts farther away in KL. This argument is separate from the Lean distance theorem.

Derive the initial KL ordering instead of memorizing it

  1. Let U(β) = Σπᵢ(β)Eᵢ. Differentiating the normalizing sum gives Z′/Z = −U.
  2. The quotient rule gives πᵢ′ = πᵢ(U−Eᵢ). Hence U′ = U²−ΣπᵢEᵢ² = −Var(E).
  3. Because the bath inverse temperature is one, ln(πᵢ(β)/πᵢ(1)) = (1−β)Eᵢ + ln Z(1)−ln Z(β).
  4. Average that expression: DKL = (1−β)U + ln Z(1)−ln Z(β).
  5. Differentiate. The −U and +U terms cancel, leaving DKL′ = (1−β)U′ = (β−1)Var(E).

For 0 < β < 1 this is strictly negative, so a hotter preparation (smaller β) starts with larger KL distance. This conclusion uses distinct energies and strictly positive Gibbs probabilities.

Step 13 · A mechanism you can see

The fourth state dilutes a negative average

The mode values are roughly (1, −0.759, −0.612, −0.004). The highest-energy state contributes almost zero to the average. At very high temperature it nevertheless holds almost one quarter of the probability. That probability dilutes the contribution from the other states.

Cool slightly: the fourth state loses probability rapidly. The negative middle-state contributions can become more prominent, making b more negative. Cool farther toward the bath: the ground state's positive contribution increases and the average returns to zero. This competition allows a small nonmonotonic part of the overlap curve.

Why is an endpoint slope enough in this special family?

Usually it is not. The proof writes J(β) = eβE₄Z(β)²b′(β), expands it into exponential terms, and shows J is strictly increasing with J(1) > 0. Thus a negative J(0) gives exactly one interior zero; a nonnegative J(0) gives none. That extra monotonicity proof rules out hidden turns. Slow route: prove the single-turn test using first-year calculus

1. Separate the numerator from its positive denominator

Use energies 0 < E₂ < E₃ < E₄, a bath at β = 1, and mode values w = (1, −p, −r, z), where p > r > −z > 0. Here p and r are positive numbers, while z is negative. This proof applies when E₄ > E₂+E₃ and (1+r)E₃ > (p−r)(E₃−E₂). These are extra hypotheses of this particular mechanism.

N(β) = 1 − p e−βE₂ − r e−βE₃ + z e−βE₄,
Z(β) = 1 + e−βE₂ + e−βE₃ + e−βE₄,   b = N/Z.

Differentiating N gives three positive terms: pE₂e−βE₂, rE₃e−βE₃, and −zE₄e−βE₄. Thus N increases strictly. Bath orthogonality says N(1) = 0. Consequently b is negative before the bath and positive after it. There is no other zero.

2. Turn the quotient rule into a sign problem

Set D = N′Z−NZ′. Since Z is positive, the quotient rule gives b′ = D/Z²; D and b′ have exactly the same sign. Expanding the two products pairs states i and j. Their contribution is (wᵢ−wⱼ)(Eⱼ−Eᵢ)e−β(Eᵢ+Eⱼ). Define six positive constants:

c₁ⱼ = (1−wⱼ)Eⱼ   for j = 2,3,4;
cᵢⱼ = (wⱼ−wᵢ)(Eⱼ−Eᵢ)   for 2 ≤ i < j ≤ 4.

The second group is positive because −p < −r < z. The extra energy inequality above is precisely c₁₃ > c₂₃. Multiply D by the positive factor eβE₄ and regroup:

J(β) = c₁₂eβ(E₄−E₂)
+ eβ(E₄−E₂−E₃)(c₁₃eβE₂−c₂₃)
+ c₁₄ − c₂₄e−βE₂ − c₃₄e−βE₃.

For β ≥ 0, the first term increases. Both factors in the second term are positive and increasing. The constant stays constant. Each of the last two negative terms increases toward zero. Therefore J is strictly increasing. Also J(1) > 0, because N(1) = 0 and N′(1) > 0.

3. Check the endpoint, including equality

J(0) = D₀ = (1−p−r+z)(E₂+E₃+E₄) + 4(pE₂+rE₃−zE₄).

If D₀ < 0, continuity gives a zero between 0 and 1, and strict increase makes that zero unique. Before it, b′ < 0. Since b < 0, the magnitude |b| increases there. Any two distinct positive β values in that interval give finite hot and cold preparations with the anomalous ordering. After the zero, |b| decreases back to zero at the bath.

If D₀ = 0, strict increase gives J(β) > 0 at every β > 0. If D₀ > 0, the same conclusion holds. Both cases exclude a cooling turn. On the heating side β > 1, both b and b′ are positive, giving normal heating order.

4. Keep the physical generator attached to the curve

A desired curve is not enough: it must come from the slowest nonconstant mode of an allowed rate matrix. In the controlled family, r = r₀+ηz, where r₀ = 48959/80000 and η = 1/160. This adjustment keeps the bath mean zero. Two rates are solved from the eigenvector equation while four stay fixed. Exact bounds show that every rate remains positive and every original triplet test fails throughout −0.03 ≤ z ≤ −0.004.

The remaining spectral check uses a baseline matrix. After converting detailed balance into a symmetric matrix, ordinary elimination rewrites its quadratic expression as a sum of four nonzero constants times squares. For the baseline shifted by 50, two constants are negative. An invertible change of variables preserves the maximum number of independent negative directions, so exactly two baseline eigenvalues lie below −50. Increasing the two varying rates only adds nonnegative squared differences to the decay expression. Those two eigenvalues therefore stay below −50. The constructed eigenvalue lies between −20 and −19; the fourth is zero. Thus the constructed mode really is the unique slow nonconstant mode.

Substitution gives D₀ = U−Vz with V > 0. Hence the exact onset is z* = U/V. Its decimal enclosure comes from rational upper and lower bounds for the logarithms of the bath weights. The separate fixed-pair threshold solves bh−bc = 0; it is different because a particular pair can miss a short interval of anomalous ordering.

5. Why the effect initially grows quadratically

Write δ = z−z* and d* = ∂D/∂β at (0,z*). The increasing-J proof gives d* > 0. Near the onset, the leading terms are D ≈ −Vδ+d*β. Solving D = 0 gives βm ≈ Vδ/d*. Because Z(0) = 4, integrate b′ ≈ (−Vδ+d*β)/16 from 0 to βm. The change in b is −V²δ²/(32d*), so its negative magnitude grows by V²δ²/(32d*). Smoothness supplies errors of order δ² in the peak location and δ³ in its height. This approximation explains the onset; the exact sign proof above establishes existence.

Step 14 · The 25% bound

Separate the difficult mode theorem from easy averaging

The difficult part of the theorem proves that the triplet premise restricts the slow mode. With a suitable sign choice:

w₁ ≥ w₂,   w₁+w₂ ≥ 2w₃,   w₄ ≤ 0,
and b(β) < 0 for 0 < β < 1.

These conditions are conclusions about a simple slow mode; simple means its eigenvalue occurs only once. They are not assumptions one is free to add to the conjecture. Their proof uses the full triplet constraints, a change of coordinates, and an elimination argument. The Lean library checks that structural step. The rest can be followed using weighted averages.

  1. Let P(β) be the total probability in states 1, 2, 3. Each has lower energy than state 4, so 3/4 < P < 1 for finite β > 0. Cooling increases P.
  2. Let m(β) be the conditional average of w within these three states: divide their weighted sum by their total probability.
  3. The first two mode inequalities imply that m cannot decrease as β increases. The expansion in the box below proves this.
  4. The full average is b = Pm + (1−P)w₄.
Check the conditional-average derivative

Write (w₁,w₂,w₃) as a constant vector plus a(1,−1,0)+c(1,1,−2), where a = (w₁−w₂)/2 ≥ 0 and c = (w₁+w₂−2w₃)/6 ≥ 0. Put x = e−βE₂, y = e−βE₃. The average of the first pattern is (1−x)/(1+x+y). Its derivative has numerator 2E₂x + E₃y − (E₃−E₂)xy > 0, since x ≤ 1. The second average is 1−3y/(1+x+y), also increasing because the third state's conditional probability decreases. Nonnegative combinations stay nondecreasing.

Prove the two claims about P

Each of the first three Boltzmann weights is strictly larger than the fourth when β > 0 and the energies are ordered. Therefore the fourth probability is less than 1/4, so P = 1−π₄ > 3/4. It is also less than one, because π₄ is positive. For monotonicity, differentiate π₄: π₄′ = π₄(U−E₄). The mean energy U is strictly below the largest energy E₄, so π₄′ < 0 and P′ > 0. All inequalities are strict for the finite admissible parameters.

Slow route: weighted coordinates explain the sign of the slow overlap

1. Replace a difficult average with simple comparisons

Here q is the bath probability vector: each qᵢ > 0 and their sum is one. Define Qₖ = q₁+⋯+qₖ. A prefix contrast compares the first k states with the next state:

h(k) = (1, …, 1, −Qₖ/qₖ₊₁, 0, …, 0).

There are k initial ones. The bath-weighted average is Qₖ−qₖ₊₁(Qₖ/qₖ₊₁) = 0. For four states, these are (1,−q₁/q₂,0,0), (1,1,−Q₂/q₃,0), and (1,1,1,−Q₃/q₄).

The weighted dot product of vectors f and g is Σqᵢfᵢgᵢ. It is the ordinary dot product after multiplying coordinate i by √qᵢ. Any two different prefix contrasts have weighted dot product zero: the longer contrast is constant on the support of the shorter, whose weighted sum is zero. Each squared length is ηₖ = QₖQₖ₊₁/qₖ₊₁. Dividing h(k) by √ηₖ produces a unit vector e(k).

These n−1 nonzero perpendicular vectors give coordinates for every vector with bath mean zero. This is just the familiar idea of perpendicular x and y axes, applied to a space with n−1 directions.

2. Translate the rate inequalities

Detailed balance makes kᵢⱼ = Mᵢⱼ/qᵢ symmetric. The triplet quantity Gᵢⱼₖ is qⱼ(kᵢₖ−kⱼₖ). Thus G ≤ 0 says kᵢₖ ≤ kⱼₖ whenever i < j < k. Write the generator acting on functions as L = Mᵀ. In the unit contrast coordinates its matrix is Aₖₗ = Σqᵢ eᵢ(k)(Le(l))ᵢ. Detailed balance makes A symmetric.

√(ηₖηₗ) Aₖₗ = Qₗ₊₁ Σᵢ≤ₖ qᵢ(kₖ₊₁,ₗ₊₁−kᵢ,ₗ₊₁)
+ Σⱼ≥ₗ₊₂ qⱼ Σᵢ≤ₖ qᵢ(kₖ₊₁,ⱼ−kᵢⱼ) ≥ 0.

Every displayed difference is nonnegative by G ≤ 0. To reproduce the multiplication, first observe that for i ≤ k+1 ≤ l, (Lh(l))ᵢ = −Qₗ₊₁kᵢ,ₗ₊₁−Σⱼ≥ₗ₊₂qⱼkᵢⱼ. Taking the shorter contrast subtracts the value at k+1 from the weighted prefix values.

3. Why nonnegative coordinates stay nonnegative

The off-diagonal entries of A are nonnegative; its diagonal entries can be negative. Choose c large enough that every entry of A+cI is nonnegative. The exponential series I+t(A+cI)+t²(A+cI)²/2!+⋯ then has nonnegative entries and diagonal entries at least one. Multiplying by e−ct shows etA has nonnegative entries and positive diagonal entries for every finite t ≥ 0. This is a direct reason that componentwise positive vectors remain positive.

The largest-eigenvalue vector of a symmetric A can be chosen nonnegative. One elementary explanation is to maximize xᵀAx over unit vectors. Replacing each coordinate by its absolute value cannot lower that expression, since its off-diagonal coefficients are nonnegative. At a maximum, the usual constrained derivative calculation gives Ax = λx. Maximizing selects the largest eigenvalue. No claim that every eigenvector has one sign is being made.

4. Compute the thermal contrast coordinates

Let gₖ(β) = Σeᵢ(k)πᵢ(β). Apart from the positive factor Z√ηₖ, its numerator is

Nₖ(β) = e−βEₖ₊₁ Σᵢ≤ₖ [eβ(Eₖ₊₁−Eᵢ)−eβb(Eₖ₊₁−Eᵢ)].

All energy gaps in brackets are positive. Thus all coordinates gₖ are negative on cooling, zero at the bath, and positive on heating. A nonzero nonnegative slow eigenvector has strictly negative dot product with g on cooling and strictly positive dot product on heating. That proves the overlap sign used in Step 14.

On heating the coordinates also increase strictly. Write Z = Nₖ+Tₖ, where Tₖ = (1+Qₖ/qₖ₊₁)e−βEₖ₊₁+Σⱼ≥ₖ₊₂e−βEⱼ. Both parts are positive. Their logarithmic derivatives satisfy Nₖ′/Nₖ > −Eₖ₊₁ and Tₖ′/Tₖ ≤ −Eₖ₊₁. The first follows from the increasing positive bracket above; the second is a weighted average of negative energies. The quotient rule now gives (Nₖ/Z)′ > 0.

5. What follows when the slow eigenvalue repeats?

A single overlap is then insufficient. Instead use the projection Π onto the entire slow eigenspace. Symmetric diagonalization gives Π = limt→∞e−λ₂tetA. The matrices inside the limit are nonnegative, so Π is nonnegative too. It is nonzero and has a positive diagonal entry. A vector whose entries are all strictly positive, or all strictly negative, therefore cannot project to zero. This excludes strong effects even with a repeated slow eigenvalue.

For heating, farther preparations have strictly larger positive g coordinates. Applying etA preserves that order. The squared weighted distance is the sum of the squares of these coordinates, so it stays strictly larger at every finite time. The leading KL coefficient is proportional to ‖Πg‖² and also increases on heating. This proves the stated all-time squared-distance and asymptotic KL conclusions. It does not establish all-time KL or total-variation order.

Slow route: derive the difficult first-three mode restriction

This route builds on the weighted-coordinate explanation immediately above. It uses algebra, inequalities, and the largest-eigenvalue idea. The new matrix-elimination step is explained along the way. Its hypotheses are four states, strictly ordered energies, positive reversible rates, every F and G nonpositive, and a simple slow eigenvalue.

1. Isolate the one inequality that needs proof

Use the unnormalized contrasts as columns of H. Write the slow mode w = Hx, where x = (x₁,x₂,x₃) has nonnegative entries. The matrix in these coordinates is B = diag(η)⁻¹Hᵀdiag(q)LH. Multiplying coordinate k by √ηₖ turns B into the symmetric matrix A above, so it has the same eigenvalues.

w₁−w₂ = (Q₂/q₂)x₁ ≥ 0,   w₄ = −(Q₃/q₄)x₃ ≤ 0,
w₁+w₂−2w₃ = (2Q₃/q₃)(x₂−sx₁),
s = (q₁−q₂)q₃/(2q₂Q₃) > 0.

Only x₂ ≥ sx₁ remains. If x₁ = 0, it already holds.

2. Subtract a common mixing process

The reduced conductances kᵢⱼ = aᵢⱼ/qᵢ have their minimum in the first row, because G ≤ 0 orders each column. Let k* be the smallest of k₁₂,k₁₃,k₁₄. Subtract the reset generator k*(q1ᵀ−Q₄I). Its off-diagonal rate into state i is k*qᵢ. All remaining rates are nonnegative, and at least one of rates 12, 13, 14 is now zero.

This operation leaves F and G unchanged: substituting aᵢⱼ−k*qᵢ makes the extra terms cancel. On every bath-mean-zero mode it merely adds k*Q₄ to the eigenvalue. Eigenvectors, ordering and simplicity are unchanged. The remaining generator may disconnect; the proof allows that.

Its eigenvalues are at most zero. To see why, detailed balance gives −⟨f,Lf⟩q = Σᵢ<ⱼ qᵢqⱼkᵢⱼ(fᵢ−fⱼ)² ≥ 0 for the remaining conductances. For an eigenvector the left side is −λ‖f‖². Thus λ ≤ 0, even when some edges vanish.

3. Eliminate the third unknown safely

All following B entries refer to the remaining generator. Put δ = λ−B₃₃, where λ is its largest contrast eigenvalue. We need δ > 0 before dividing. Symmetry implies λ ≥ B₃₃: test the quadratic expression on the third unit vector. If equality held, the third diagonal entry of λI−A would be zero. This matrix has nonnegative quadratic expression; any nonzero off-diagonal entry in that row would make the expression negative for a suitable small perturbation. Its whole third row must therefore vanish. The third unit vector would be a largest-eigenvalue vector. Simplicity would force x₁ = 0, contrary to the case under consideration. Hence δ > 0.

The third equation gives x₃ = (B₃₁x₁+B₃₂x₂)/δ. Substitute it into the first two equations. The resulting two-by-two matrix is Eᵢⱼ = Bᵢⱼ+Bᵢ₃B₃ⱼ/δ, for i,j = 1,2.

Why is λ still its simple largest eigenvalue? In symmetric coordinates, complete the square in the third variable of the nonnegative quadratic expression for λI−A. The remaining two-variable expression is exactly the symmetric version of λI−E. It is nonnegative. Completing the square is an invertible change of variables, and the eliminated square has positive coefficient δ, so the dimension of the zero space remains one. This gives both largest-eigenvalue ordering and simplicity.

4. Turn the eigenvector ratio into a polynomial sign

f(s) = E₂₁+s(E₂₂−E₁₁)−s²E₁₂,
δf(s) = δC+D*,
C = B₂₁+s(B₂₂−B₁₁)−s²B₁₂,
D* = (B₂₃−sB₁₃)(B₃₁+sB₃₂).

If the off-diagonal entries of E are positive, its eigenvector ratio x₂/x₁ is the unique positive zero of f. The quadratic begins positive at zero and opens downward. Therefore f(s) ≥ 0 implies s ≤ x₂/x₁. If one off-diagonal entry vanishes, symmetry after positive rescaling makes both vanish. Simplicity and x₁ > 0 then give x₂ = 0 and E₁₁ > E₂₂, so f(s) < 0. Thus showing f(s) ≥ 0 also excludes this exceptional case.

The G inequalities give B₃₁,B₃₂ ≥ 0 and

B₂₃−sB₁₃ = [Q₄/(2q₁q₂Q₃)](2a₃₄q₁q₂−a₁₄q₂q₃−a₂₄q₁q₃) ≥ 0.

For the last sign, G₁₃₄ ≤ 0 and G₂₃₄ ≤ 0 bound each of the two subtracted terms by a₃₄q₁q₂. Hence D* ≥ 0. If C ≥ 0, we are done. If C < 0, use λ ≤ 0 to get δC+D* ≥ −B₃₃C+D*. The unknown eigenvalue has disappeared.

5. Check every zero-rate boundary

Call the six remaining downward rates (a,b,c,d,e,f) = (a₁₂,a₁₃,a₁₄,a₂₃,a₂₄,a₃₄). Here the rate f is unrelated to the polynomial f(s) above. Direct expansion gives

−B₃₃C+D* = [Q₂Q₄/(4q₁q₂²Q₃²)]T,
T = (c+e+f)N+q₄[2ceq₃(q₁−q₂)+fJ],
N = −2q₁q₂q₃F₁₂₃ ≥ 0,
J = eq₁(2q₂+q₃)−cq₂(2q₁+q₃).

The prefactor is positive. At least one of a,b,c is zero; this is exactly why we subtracted the reset process.

  1. c = 0: J = eq₁(2q₂+q₃) ≥ 0. Every term in T is nonnegative.
  2. a = 0: F₁₂₄ ≤ 0 gives eq₁(2q₂+q₄)−cq₂(2q₁+q₄) ≥ 0. Multiply this by (2q₁+q₃)/(2q₁+q₄), then add 2eq₁(q₁−q₂)(q₃−q₄)/(2q₁+q₄). The result is exactly J. Both pieces are nonnegative, including when c or e is zero. Thus T ≥ 0.
  3. b = 0: N = 2aq₂q₃(q₁−q₂)+dq₁q₃(2q₂+q₃). If c ≤ e, write J = (e−c)q₁(2q₂+q₃)+cq₃(q₁−q₂) ≥ 0. If c > e, use the identity N+q₄J = 2q₁q₂(q₃−q₄)(c−e)+dq₁q₃(2q₂+q₃)−2q₁q₂q₃F₁₂₄ ≥ 0. Now T = (c+e)N+f(N+q₄J)+2ceq₃q₄(q₁−q₂) ≥ 0.

1. Keep two different constructions separate

2. A continuity argument gives actual positive parameters

For the simpler family, set rate a₁₂ = 1 and define the other rates by the rational functions in the formal sharpness appendix. A rational function is a polynomial divided by a polynomial. If its denominator is nonzero at ε = 0, its value changes continuously near zero. If its value there is positive, it remains positive on some interval around zero.

The proof needs thirteen such functions to be positive simultaneously. They control the three polynomial denominators, five varying rates, three F signs and two G signs. Their values at zero, in the appendix's order, are

Two triplet expressions, F₁₂₄ and G₂₃₄, are exactly zero. Another, G₁₂₄, tends to zero. Its sign is established by factoring out ε: −G₁₂₄ is ε times the thirteenth positive function. 3. Verify that −3 is the simple slow eigenvalue

The three contrast coordinates of w are positive. The contrast matrix B has nonnegative off-diagonal entries, and the two entries connecting its first coordinate to each of the other two are strictly positive. Here is a useful direct algebraic test for simplicity.

Let A = diag(η)(−3I−B). It is symmetric, Ax = 0 for the positive coordinate vector x, and its off-diagonal entries are nonpositive. Expanding the squares gives, for every y,

yᵀAy = Σᵢ<ⱼ (−Aᵢⱼ)xᵢxⱼ(yᵢ/xᵢ−yⱼ/xⱼ)² ≥ 0.

Hence every zero vector of A is a multiple of x: the eigenvalue is simple. All nonstationary modes lie in this contrast space, so this is the desired slow-mode statement for the full generator.

The parameter ε = e−L² is positive for each finite L and tends to zero as L grows. It eventually enters the interval where every rate and sign check holds. At the same time, L > 1 gives 0 < L−3 < L−1 < 1, so both preparations are finite and on the cooling side.

Step 17 · Continuous verification

Checking many points does not check every point

There are infinitely many points in a box of possible parameters. Even checking all corners can miss an interior failure. Interval arithmetic replaces a number with a lower and upper bound and carries safe bounds through each operation. If a full small box proves the required sign, every point in it passes. If it cannot, split the box and try again.

Example: x ∈ [1,2], y ∈ [3,4] ⇒ x+y ∈ [4,6].
But x−x may be enclosed as [−1,1], even though its actual value is 0.

When ordinary ordering is preserved at every time

1. Follow adjacent differences

For values f₁,…,fₙ on energy-ordered states, define yᵢ = fᵢ₊₁−fᵢ. The function is increasing exactly when every yᵢ ≥ 0. The generator L = Mᵀ acts on functions. Because L sends constant functions to zero, its action on differences is described by an (n−1)-by-(n−1) matrix K.

If every off-diagonal entry of K is nonnegative, the exponential-series argument from the contrast lesson gives etK ≥ 0. Therefore an initially increasing function stays increasing after evolution. This property is called stochastic monotonicity. It is stronger than merely having the contrast matrix from Step 14 nonnegative off the diagonal: the two matrices use different coordinates.

For example, one required four-state entry is K₂₁ = a₁₂−a₁₃. It equals −27 in both counterexamples here, so they do not have this ordering property. There is no conflict between them and a theorem that assumes it.

2. Translate function order into probability order

A probability distribution p is stochastically above q if the average of every increasing function is at least as large under p. For ordered discrete states, this says p has at least as much probability above every cutoff. Hotter Gibbs preparations have this order because increasing temperature shifts weight toward higher energies. A semigroup preserving increasing functions also preserves this order between distributions.

For a reversible process, the density ratio pᵢ/qᵢ evolves by the same function semigroup. On cooling, a Gibbs preparation divided by the bath distribution is increasing with energy, and it stays increasing. This is the extra fact needed to compare distances.

3. Use the supporting-line inequality for a convex function

A convex curve lies above each of its supporting lines. If g(y) is the slope of a supporting line at y, then Φ(x)−Φ(y) ≥ g(y)(x−y), and slopes can be chosen nondecreasing. Apply this to the density ratios of farther and nearer preparations and multiply by the bath probabilities:

DΦ(pfar‖q)−DΦ(pnear‖q)
≥ Σᵢ(pfar,i−pnear,i) g(pnear,i/qᵢ) ≥ 0.

On cooling, the last slope vector is increasing, so stochastic order gives the last inequality. On heating, both relevant orders reverse, leaving the same conclusion. With Φ(x) = x ln x this is KL divergence; with Φ(x) = |x−1|/2 it is total variation. KL is strictly convex, and finite-time matrix evolution is invertible, so distinct preparations have strict KL ordering at every finite time.

This argument does not need a simple slow mode. The separate asymptotic proof does: a nonnegative eigenvector for K lifts to a monotone slow function, whose Gibbs covariance derivative has a strict sign.

The ordering principle has established precedents: Baake and Sumner, Proposition 3.11, and Ohga, Hayakawa and Ito, Result 2 and SI D2. The latter's stated no-Mpemba result concerns energy; the supporting-line derivation above supplies the convex-divergence conclusion under the assumptions stated here.

How the energies alone can rule out cooling in the larger contrast family

1. A decreasing logarithmic slope cannot turn upward

Return to the contrast coordinates gₖ from Step 14. On cooling Rₖ = −gₖ is positive. Apart from a fixed positive factor, it is a decaying exponential times

[αₖ−Σᵢ≤ₖ eβ(Eₖ₊₁−Eᵢ)]/Z(β),
αₖ = Σᵢ≤ₖ eβb(Eₖ₊₁−Eᵢ).

The bracket is positive before the bath, with negative first and second derivatives. Its logarithm has negative second derivative. Also (ln Z)″ is the positive energy variance. Thus (ln Rₖ)″ < 0. If Rₖ starts with a nonpositive derivative at β = 0, its logarithmic slope becomes strictly negative for every later cooling β. This includes an initially zero derivative.

2. Reduce all starting slopes to one energy test

Shift E₁ to zero, put a = E₂, and let μ = (E₁+⋯+Eₙ)/n be the ordinary arithmetic mean energy. For the first contrast, g₁′(0) has the sign of μ−eβba(μ−a). If μ ≤ a, this is positive. Otherwise it is nonnegative exactly when

βb ≤ β* = ln[μ/(μ−a)]/a.

This one condition controls every later contrast too. For y = Eₖ₊₁ ≥ μ, the required starting slope is positive because a proper initial segment of the energy list has mean below μ. For y < μ, compare averages of the increasing function 1/(μ−t). Its average over [Eᵢ,y] is at least its average over [0,a]. Integrating gives

ln[(μ−Eᵢ)/(μ−y)]/(y−Eᵢ) ≥ ln[μ/(μ−a)]/a.

Exponentiation now bounds each term in the starting slope in the required direction. All cooling contrast magnitudes therefore decrease toward the bath. The nonnegative contrast evolution preserves their componentwise order, excluding finite-time weighted squared-distance reversal. The slow-projection argument also excludes asymptotic KL reversal.

3. Why this threshold is exact for the stated family

If βb > β*, the first contrast initially increases in magnitude. It can be made the simple slow mode by adding a small rank-one term to a reset generator. All G constraints then hold at equality, and finite positive preparations sufficiently near β = 0 witness cooling. This establishes sharpness for the G-only family. It does not assert that those rates satisfy the extra F constraints.

For four equally spaced energies, μ = 3a/2, so the threshold is βba ≤ ln 3. A spacing-independent consequence for n ≥ 3 is βb(Eₙ−E₁) ≤ n/(n−2). In four states the latter cutoff is 2. The proof bounds μ by placing every energy above E₂ at the highest energy and expands the resulting logarithms; every correction beyond n/(n−2) is positive. Distinct energies approaching that limiting arrangement show the constant cannot be improved for the G-only family.

4. The G-only amplitude limit is larger

Each contrast magnitude has a decreasing positive numerator. Its cold-to-hot ratio is therefore strictly less than the hot-to-cold denominator ratio. If the contrast compares the first m−1 states with state m, the first m terms of the cold denominator contribute at least m, and the other n−m hot terms contribute at most one each. The ratio is below n/m ≤ n/2. The slow-mode ratio is a nonnegative weighted average of these contrast ratios, so it too is below n/2.

This bound can be approached. Use energies 0, 1/L, L, 2L,… and the first contrast as the slow mode. With βh = L−2 and βc = L−1/2, the hot partition function tends to n and the cold one tends to 2; the two overlap numerators are both asymptotic to −1/L. The ratio tends to n/2. Thus the G-only amplitude supremum is 1−2/n, or 50% for four states. The full F-and-G premise gives the stronger 25% result proved earlier.

Why the general four-state test needs only endpoints and one possible extra point

1. Turn four derivatives into a curve in a plane

Use energies (0,a,b,c), with 0 < a < b < c. The ground-state probability increases with β, so g = π₁′ > 0. Define u = π₂′/g and v = π₃′/g. Since the four probability derivatives sum to zero, any fixed mode w has

b′(β) = g(β)Φ(β),
Φ = A+Bu+Cv,
A = w₁−w₄,   B = w₂−w₄,   C = w₃−w₄.

Only the sign of Φ matters. As β varies, (u,v) traces a curve. Exact differentiation shows u decreases strictly and v, considered as a function of u, has strictly positive curvature. Therefore a line A+Bu+Cv = 0 intersects it at most twice, counting a tangency twice.

2. Check the determinant signs behind that claim

A Wronskian is a determinant whose rows contain functions and their successive derivatives. It measures whether those functions can locally mimic one another. For g, gu and gv, elementary determinant operations give

W(g,gu) = g²u′,
W(g,gu,gv) = g³(u′)³ d²v/du².

Both determinants are negative for four ordered energies. To see the signs without trusting a graph, first add the constant function to replace derivatives of probabilities by probabilities. Multiply each column function by the positive partition function Z; the product rule changes the determinant by the positive factor Z to the number of columns. The columns now contain simple exponentials. Their derivatives multiply them by powers of −Eᵢ, producing a Vandermonde determinant: a product of energy differences. Expanding the Z column leaves only positive exponential weights times those signed products. The two required products have negative signs. Hence u′ < 0 and d²v/du² > 0.

3. Find the only possible interior extremum of Φ

Put c₀ = ab(b−a), c₁ = ac(c−a), c₂ = bc(c−b), γ = (c−a)/(c−b) > 1, and x = e−(c−b)β. The slope of the curve is

s(x) = dv/du = (c₂xγ−c₀)/(c₀+c₁x).

Its derivative has positive numerator c₀c₂γxγ−1+c₁c₂(γ−1)xγ+c₀c₁. Thus s increases strictly with x, from s(0) = −1 to s(1). If C ≠ 0, an interior extremum of Φ must solve s(x*) = −B/C. There is exactly one such solution when −1 < −B/C < s(1), and none otherwise. Equality at either endpoint is excluded. When C = 0, Φ is already monotone or constant.

For example, energies (0,1,2,3) give s(x) = (6x²−2)/(2+6x). If −B/C = −1/3, the unique solution is x* = 1/3 and β* = ln 3. One scalar equation replaces an unlimited temperature scan.

4. Test signs, not just zeros

On cooling, evaluate Φ at β = 0, at the bath, and at β* if it lies strictly between them. On heating, evaluate it at the bath, at infinity using Φ(∞) = w₁−w₂, and at β* if it lies on that side. A side has an ordinary asymptotic reversal exactly when its list contains both a strictly positive and a strictly negative value.

Why both signs? If b′ keeps one sign, b is monotone and its zero at the bath forces normal magnitude order. If b′ takes both signs on cooling, consider its last sign-changing zero before the bath. Just after that zero, b must head toward its bath value zero; just before it, b has the same nonzero sign but its derivative has the opposite sign. Its magnitude is therefore increasing there. The heating proof uses the first sign-changing zero after the bath. This produces a finite interval containing actual finite-temperature witnesses.

A tangent zero without opposite signs is insufficient. A zero exactly at β = 0 or infinity is a limiting statement, not a finite preparation. For a strong effect, additionally find zeros of b itself: split at the stationary points, check each strictly monotone interval for opposite endpoint signs, and count stationary zero points separately. General transcendental equalities require proof or rigorous enclosures; floating-point tolerances do not decide them.

When several slow patterns tie: project onto the whole space

1. Replace one coefficient by a vector

Suppose two independent patterns both decay as eλt. Rotating the coordinate axes within their plane changes the individual coefficients, but not the total slow component. A vector (3,4) can become (5,0) after a rotation; the zero coordinate does not mean the vector disappeared. The same issue occurs with a repeated slow eigenvalue.

Divide each probability deviation by √qᵢ, where q is the bath distribution. Detailed balance makes the generator symmetric in these coordinates. Let P be the orthogonal projection onto its entire slow eigenspace. The relevant nonnegative amplitude is

Q(β) = ‖P D−1/2(π(β)−q)‖² = π(β)ᵀHπ(β),
H = D−1/2P D−1/2.

The bath disappears because P√q = 0. The leading chi-square distance is e2λtQ(β), and leading KL is half of it. A strong preparation requires Q = 0, meaning the entire projected vector vanishes. Equal nonzero Q values for a specified pair leave the subleading terms to decide their ordering.

2. Count how many thermal preparations can disappear

With n distinct energies, any k ≤ n distinct Gibbs vectors are linearly independent. To see why, multiply each by its positive partition function. A hypothetical dependence would make a nonzero combination of k distinct exponentials vanish at k different temperatures. Divide by one exponential and differentiate: one term disappears. Repeating Rolle’s theorem gives at most k−1 zeros, a contradiction.

If the slow space has dimension r, the kernel of its projection has dimension n−r. It already contains the transformed bath vector. It can therefore contain at most n−r−1 additional independent Gibbs vectors. For four states, the maximum numbers of nonbath strong preparations are 2, 1, and 0 for slow multiplicities 1, 2, and 3.

3. Why the bound can be reached

Choose the desired preparations and span them together with the bath. Project onto the perpendicular complement of this span, then use S = −I + ssᵀ + εP, with s = √q and a sufficiently small positive ε. Its slow space is exactly the chosen complement. Small ε preserves positive transition rates because the unperturbed reset has strictly positive rates. This construction establishes the bound in the unrestricted reversible family; it does not promise the triplet-failure premise.

4. The full triplet premise is much more restrictive

If n ≥ 4 and only one nonstationary mode is faster than all the others, the full F-and-G conditions force the generator to be a reset plus mixing on one adjacent pair. The fast vector is supported only on that pair. The proof uses the last nonzero component to order the earlier components with G, then F forces all but its adjacent predecessor to vanish.

For four states this covers all repeated-slow cases, and stochastic order gives normal convex-divergence ordering at every time. For five or more states, smaller repeated slow spaces can have multiple faster modes; the same classification cannot be assumed. An exact five-state example has a negative adjacent-difference matrix entry despite all F and G being nonpositive.

A finite-time crossing can disappear again: solve the three-exponential test

1. Subtract the two squared distances

In the symmetric coordinates, different eigenspaces are perpendicular. Squaring the length therefore gives no cross terms. Group repeated eigenvalues together and subtract the nearer preparation from the farther one:

Δ(t) = Σⱼ Aⱼ e2μⱼt,
Aⱼ = ‖Pⱼzfar‖² − ‖Pⱼznear‖².

There are at most three distinct nonzero eigenvalues in four states. A coefficient Aⱼ can vanish even when both individual projections are nonzero; discard that exact zero before identifying the dominant remaining exponential. For two different Gibbs preparations on the same bath side, Δ(0) is strictly positive.

2. Factor out a positive exponential

With three remaining terms the sign is the sign of F(t) = A + Be−at + Ce−bt, where 0 < a < b. Differentiating gives F′ = −aBe−at − bCe−bt. A positive stationary time exists only when BC < 0 and −bC/(aB) > 1. If it exists, it is

t* = ln[−bC/(aB)] / (b−a).

Check F(0), the limiting constant A, and F(t*) when that time is admissible. A negative minimum proves a reversal. A zero minimum does not: the graph may only touch zero or approach it forever. With two terms, the factored expression is monotone, so only endpoints are needed.

3. A hand-solvable illustration

Let x = e−t and F = 1−6x+6x². At t = 0, x = 1 and F = 1. At very late times x tends to zero and F tends to 1 again. But at x = 1/2, F = −1/2. Solving the quadratic gives x = (3 ± √3)/6. Both roots lie strictly between zero and one, producing two finite crossings and a reversed interval between them.

1. An attractive curve is not yet a realizable model

You can write down values w₁,…,w₄ that produce an interesting thermal overlap. To realize the pattern, it must be an eigenfunction of a probability-conserving reversible matrix, be its simple slowest nonstationary mode, and satisfy all the rate constraints. The eigenvalue equation alone does not guarantee the last two requirements.

2. Design the two faster directions first

Subtract the bath mean from w, so qᵀw = 0. Choose two independent columns U spanning the directions perpendicular to both the constant function and w in the bath-weighted inner product. For a symmetric two-by-two matrix B, form the signed auxiliary matrix M₀ = −DUBUᵀ. It kills the stationary and proposed slow directions. 3. The remaining problem is a disk and straight lines

Positive rescaling lets us set trace B = 1:

B = ½ [[1+x, y], [y, 1−x]].
Its eigenvalues are (1 ± √(x²+y²))/2.
Both are positive exactly when x²+y² < 1.

Why one endpoint decides cooling under the full triplet premise

1. Keep the premises visible

This shortcut assumes all four original triplet predicates fail and the slow mode is simple. It does not apply to an arbitrary four-state mode. In the contrast orientation the cooling overlap b is negative, and b′ at the bath is strictly positive.

2. Split according to w₃−w₄

If C = w₃−w₄ is nonnegative, the conditional first-three mean is increasing and stays above w₄. Differentiating b = Pm+(1−P)w₄ gives b′ = Pm′+P′(m−w₄) > 0, so there is no cooling anomaly.

If C < 0, use the derivative curve from the preceding explanation: b′ = gΦ with g > 0 and Φ = A+Bu+Cv(u). Because v(u) is strictly convex, Φ is strictly concave. At the low-temperature endpoint its limit is w₁−w₂ ≥ 0. At the bath it is positive. If its infinite-temperature-end value is also nonnegative, the whole interior lies above the nonnegative chord and has positive derivative.

If that last endpoint value is negative, concavity permits exactly one crossing before reaching the bath. Thus b′ is negative first, then positive forever. Since b is negative on cooling, its absolute value first grows and then shrinks. This gives a unique maximum and finite hot preparations with reversed ordering.

Cooling exists ⇔ b′(0) < 0 ⇔ Covuniform(w,E) > 0.

Here β = 0 is an analytic endpoint used to decide the sign. It is not presented as a finite-temperature witness. Strict negativity persists at nearby positive β. Endpoint equality instead gives strict normal ordering at every finite positive β.

3. Avoid choosing an eigenvector by using a projector

ℛ(r) / p′(r) = b(0)b′(0) / (wᵀDw).

The denominator on the right is positive, p′(r) > 0, and b(0) < 0. Therefore ℛ(r) > 0 means cooling. How exact algorithms decide signs and zeros without a numerical tolerance

1. Turn commensurate energies into polynomials

If Eᵢ = mᵢΔ with integer mᵢ, set x = e−βΔ. Thermal weights become xmᵢ. After shifting all exponents equally, the partition function Z and slow numerator N are polynomials. The derivative sign is the sign of N′Z−NZ′ for a simple mode, or N′Z−2NZ′ for the squared projection amplitude.

Factor this polynomial by multiplicity. A root of odd multiplicity changes sign; a root of even multiplicity merely touches zero. Count the odd roots on the open cooling and heating intervals. Count all distinct numerator roots separately for strong preparations. Remove the bath and the zero/infinite-temperature endpoint factors exactly.

2. Algebraic coefficients need the correct real root

A cubic can define several real numbers. Saying “α is a root” is insufficient: an isolating rational interval selects which one. A polynomial expression in α may have a negative value even if its leading coefficient is positive. For example, the real root α of α³−α−1 = 0 lies between 1 and 2, so α−2 is negative.

The implementation first reduces expressions exactly to detect zero. For a nonzero expression it evaluates rational intervals and refines the selected root interval until zero is excluded. The Sturm sequence counts roots using these correctly embedded signs. It does not infer signs from the appearance of a coefficient list.

3. Incommensurate rational weights lead to prime logarithms

With arbitrary rational bath weights, the full-premise endpoint scalar is a sum of algebraic coefficients times logarithms of rational numbers. Factor those rationals into primes to write ℛ(r) = Σγₚ ln p. Unique prime factorization makes the prime logarithms independent over rational coefficients. The cited form of Baker’s theorem then makes them linearly independent over algebraic coefficients. Consequently the sum is zero exactly when every γₚ is zero. This uses an established theorem; it does not assert algebraic independence of logarithms.

If some coefficient is nonzero, convergent rational logarithm bounds eventually determine the sign. Range reduction keeps the series arguments small. A resource cap can stop refinement early, but the returned answer must then be “unresolved,” not “no cooling.” The tests exercise both exact equality and small opposite signs that need more than one refinement round.

Why an ordered chain of contrasts preserves every convex heating distance

1. Start with an average that cannot be negative

If two quantities increase together, their covariance is nonnegative. For values aᵢ and an increasing function g, with positive weights pᵢ summing to one, expand the two sides to check

Cov(a,g(a)) = ½ ΣᵢΣⱼ pᵢpⱼ(aᵢ−aⱼ)(g(aᵢ)−g(aⱼ)) ≥ 0.

Each product is nonnegative because its two factors have the same sign. For a convex differentiable function Φ, the slope Φ′ is increasing, so we can use g = Φ′. Strict convexity makes the covariance positive whenever at least two a values differ.

2. Apply that fact to a prefix of physical states

Write the density ratio rᵢ = pᵢ/qᵢ as r = 1+Hx, where H contains the unnormalized prefix contrasts. On heating, all x coordinates are positive. Put gₖ = ΣᵢqᵢHᵢₖΦ′(rᵢ); this is the derivative of the divergence with respect to coordinate xₖ. Individual gₖ need not be positive. Instead look at cumulative sums Sₖ = Σⱼ≤ₖ xⱼgⱼ.

Higher contrasts are constant on states 1 through k+1. Their combined contribution there is cₖ = 1+Σⱼ>ₖxⱼ. The lower contrasts have zero weighted mean on that prefix. Therefore

Sₖ = Σᵢ≤ₖ₊₁ qᵢ(rᵢ−cₖ)Φ′(rᵢ) ≥ 0.

This is exactly prefix mass times the covariance just derived. Notice the distinction: cumulative sums are positive even when individual terms can be negative.

3. Learn the small summation trick that uses those prefixes

Let y = ∂x/∂β and Rₖ = yₖ/xₖ. If R₁ ≥ R₂ ≥ … > 0, then collecting the coefficient of each xₖgₖ gives

ΣₖRₖxₖgₖ = Rn−1Sn−1 + Σₖ<ₙ₋₁(Rₖ−Rₖ₊₁)Sₖ ≥ 0.

This is summation by parts. For three terms, expand R₃S₃+(R₂−R₃)S₂+(R₁−R₂)S₁ to recover the original sum. The identity turns information about partial sums into information about the weighted total.

4. Explain why the ratios keep their order

Initially each xₖ is proportional to δ times an integral of Q(s)e−δs, truncated at Eₖ₊₁ and divided by the relative partition function. Here δ = β−βb > 0 and Q(s) is the bath mass below energy s. Differentiating its logarithm gives a common term minus the mean s in that truncated integral. Extending the upper endpoint increases that mean, so Rₖ decreases with k.

A tridiagonal Metzler contrast generator only couples neighboring contrast coordinates, with nonnegative off-diagonal entries. Its exponential has nonnegative ordered 2×2 determinants. The determinant expansion then preserves decreasing yₖ/xₖ ratios. One can prove the determinant claim by differentiating those determinants: their evolution again has nonnegative off-diagonal coefficients because a neighboring index cannot cross another occupied index.

This condition concerns contrast coordinates, not neighboring physical energy states. It is also a real restriction: a general G-admissible generator can destroy the ratio order. The resulting proof-route failure is not itself a KL reversal.

5. Keep track of strictness and nonsmooth distances

At finite time a distinct initial state cannot become exactly equilibrium, because a matrix exponential is invertible. Thus the full covariance is positive for strictly convex Φ, and the last R is positive. Integrating the positive thermal derivative proves strict heating order. For nonsmooth convex functions, ordered subgradients replace Φ′ almost everywhere; local Lipschitz continuity permits integration. Non-strict convexity gives the non-strict conclusion.

A guaranteed KL window: turn temperature distance into an angle estimate

1. Form a dimensionless measure of how far you start from the bath

Let δ = |β−βb| and c = Emax−Emin. The product z = δc is dimensionless: inverse temperature times energy. Small z means the Gibbs weights have not spread very far relative to the bath. The result guarantees ordinary KL ordering on either side whenever z ≤ 2/√5, approximately 0.8944. Cooling still requires positive initial β.

2. Compare a vector with the direction in which it is growing

After choosing the outward sign, write the positive contrast coordinates as x and their outward thermal derivative as y. Integral formulas for the initial Gibbs weights give, coordinate by coordinate,

(1/δ−c)x ≤ y ≤ (1/δ+c)x.

The nonnegative contrast semigroup preserves both inequalities at every time. Thus y points broadly in the same direction as x. More precisely, if a = 1/δ−c and b = 1/δ+c, the cosine of their angle is at least 2√(ab)/(a+b) = √(1−z²), provided z < 1.

To derive this without guessing an angle, put vᵢ = yᵢ/xᵢ and assign weight xᵢ²/‖x‖². Since a ≤ vᵢ ≤ b, the inequality (vᵢ−a)(vᵢ−b) ≤ 0 gives E[v²] ≤ (a+b)E[v]−ab. The squared cosine is E[v]²/E[v²]. Its lower bound reduces to the nonnegative square ((a+b)E[v]−2ab)².

3. A positive matrix can change the angle, but only by a bounded amount

KL is curved, rather than exactly a squared Euclidean distance. Its Hessian describes that curvature. Along the straight line from equilibrium to the evolved density r, the integrated Hessian T has all eigenvalues between m = 1/rmax and M = 1/rmin. The initial density range has rmax/rmin = ez, and Markov averaging keeps the evolved density inside that range.

The outward KL derivative is yᵀTx. Split T into its scalar center (M+m)I/2 and a remainder of norm at most (M−m)/2. The scalar part rewards alignment; the remainder can reduce the result only by its norm times ‖x‖‖y‖. Hence

∂DKL/∂δ ≥ ½(M+m)[√(1−z²)−tanh(z/2)]‖x‖‖y‖.

4. Check the advertised endpoint, rather than assuming a small-parameter approximation

For 0 < z ≤ 2/√5, squaring the nonnegative sides gives √(1−z²) ≥ z/2. Also tanh(u) < u for positive u, so the bracket is strictly positive even at the upper endpoint. Both vectors remain nonzero at every finite time. The bath endpoint itself has zero KL; it does not require substituting δ = 0 into 1/δ.

This is an exact sufficient bound, uniform in time, rather than a truncated Taylor expansion. Repeated eigenvalues do not invalidate it. Beyond this window the proof becomes inconclusive; it does not predict a reversal. The numerical audit supports the proof on sampled cases, but its finite samples cannot establish the universal theorem.

1. Combine contributions only after checking their signs

The contrast theorem writes the oriented slow mode as a nonnegative combination of prefix contrasts. Group its first two contrasts into f, supported on the first three states. If the additional inequality w₁+w₂ ≥ 2w₃ holds, f has a conditional thermal average that strictly increases toward its bath value zero. On cooling that average is negative.

Its full overlap is the conditional mean times the probability P₃ of being in the first three states. At finite positive β, 3/n < P₃ < 1. Therefore its cold-to-hot magnitude ratio is less than n/3: the conditional magnitude shrinks and the probability factor grows by less than n/3.

Each later contrast similarly has a ratio less than n/(k+1), hence at most the stronger threshold n/4. All nonzero components are negative on cooling, so no cancellation occurs. The ratio of their sum is a weighted average of their ratios, with hot magnitudes as positive weights. It is consequently below n/3.

Additional arithmetic inequality ⇒ amplitude advantage < 1−3/n.
For n > 4, the full triplet premise has not yet been proved to imply that additional inequality.

2. One special tail can be compressed exactly for this purpose

Direct rate identities preserve every F and G inequality: the new F is an average of old F values plus nonnegative multiples of old nonpositive G values. The established four-state theorem then supplies the missing arithmetic inequality.

Full dynamic lumpability is unnecessary because only this eigenfunction is being retained. But a constant tail and a sufficiently small total tail weight are essential assumptions. This averaging is different from eliminating a state through a Schur complement.

3. Convert a proposed mode into a graph construction

How a computer proves a whole parameter box instead of sampling it

1. Replace a guessed number with an enclosure

A floating-point calculation returns a nearby representable number. An interval calculation returns two numbers guaranteed to lie below and above the exact answer. For example, if x ∈ [2,3] and y ∈ [4,5], then x+y ∈ [6,8] and xy ∈ [8,15]. The guarantee concerns every allowed pair, including pairs you never sampled.

Computer endpoints themselves are rounded. The implementation widens each basic arithmetic result to the neighboring representable value in the outward direction. It uses MPFR directed rounding for decimal inputs, exponentials, logarithms, and square roots. These guarantees rely on the stated arithmetic environment and compilation settings; the compiler is instructed not to use fast-math or fused contraction.

A denominator interval containing zero does not justify division. A logarithm interval reaching zero does not justify taking a logarithm. Those cases become unresolved calculations that need narrower cells. They never become successful certificates by ignoring the invalid part.

2. Understand why a valid enclosure can still be too wide

If x ∈ [0,1], ordinary interval subtraction gives x−x ∈ [−1,1], although the true result is exactly zero. The enclosure is valid but loses the fact that both appearances represent the same x. This dependency problem can make a correct method fail to certify a true inequality.

The physical checker reduces this overestimation in several ways. It transforms constant edge matrices before multiplying uncertain rates, preserving useful cancellations. It also computes a value at the box center and adds derivative bounds times coordinate displacements:

f(box) ⊆ f(center) + Σₖ [∂f/∂xₖ](box)·(xₖ−centerₖ).

This is the multivariable mean-value bound. Intersecting it with a direct interval evaluation is safe when both enclose the same function. A narrower intersection is stronger evidence; an empty intersection is an error or unresolved calculation, never proof of the desired claim.

3. Reduce the dynamics without discarding a state

Probability deviations sum to zero, so the fourth deviation equals minus the first three. This leaves three independent coordinates. The basis is near a numerical eigenbasis, but the proof does not assume its entries are exact eigenvectors. Its decimal entries and inverse are enclosed directly.

1. Start with one adjustable number

Suppose you can choose a number x between 0 and 1. For each choice, a calculation gives a score f(x). You want to know whether the score stays positive. Trying x = 0 and x = 1 gives two answers. It does not yet tell you what happens between them.

Take f(x) = (x−½)²−¹⁄₁₀. At either endpoint, f = ¼−¹⁄₁₀ = ³⁄₂₀ = 0.15. At the middle, f(½) = −0.1. Both endpoint tests pass, while an interior test fails. The issue is not that the computer calculated incorrectly. The tested points did not cover the claim.

2. Add a second adjustable number

Two parameters make a rectangle of possible choices. Checking its four corners still says nothing about an arbitrary nonlinear function inside it. With eight parameters there are 2⁸ = 256 corners, but infinitely many interior points. A corner reduction requires an additional mathematical argument, such as suitable monotonicity in every parameter. Counting corners is not that argument.

Checking one parameter at a time is weaker still. For f(x,y) = 0.1−xy with x and y between −1 and 1, each coordinate axis has f = 0.1. Changing both parameters to 1 gives f = −0.9. Interactions can hide entirely from single-parameter tests.
