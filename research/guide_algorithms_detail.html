When several slow patterns tie: project onto the whole space

1. Replace one coefficient by a vector

Suppose two independent patterns both decay as eλt. Rotating the coordinate axes within their plane changes the individual coefficients, but not the total slow component. A vector (3,4) can become (5,0) after a rotation; the zero coordinate does not mean the vector disappeared. The same issue occurs with a repeated slow eigenvalue.

Divide each probability deviation by √qᵢ, where q is the bath distribution. Detailed balance makes the generator symmetric in these coordinates. Let P be the orthogonal projection onto its entire slow eigenspace. The relevant nonnegative amplitude is

Q(β) = ‖P D−1/2(π(β)−q)‖² = π(β)ᵀHπ(β),
H = D−1/2P D−1/2.

The bath disappears because P√q = 0. The leading chi-square distance is e2λtQ(β), and leading KL is half of it. A strong preparation requires Q = 0, meaning the entire projected vector vanishes. Equal nonzero Q values for a specified pair leave the subleading terms to decide their ordering.

2. Count how many thermal preparations can disappear

With n distinct energies, any k ≤ n distinct Gibbs vectors are linearly independent. To see why, multiply each by its positive partition function. A hypothetical dependence would make a nonzero combination of k distinct exponentials vanish at k different temperatures. Divide by one exponential and differentiate: one term disappears. Repeating Rolle’s theorem gives at most k−1 zeros, a contradiction.

If the slow space has dimension r, the kernel of its projection has dimension n−r. It already contains the transformed bath vector. It can therefore contain at most n−r−1 additional independent Gibbs vectors. For four states, the maximum numbers of nonbath strong preparations are 2, 1, and 0 for slow multiplicities 1, 2, and 3.

3. Why the bound can be reached

Choose the desired preparations and span them together with the bath. Project onto the perpendicular complement of this span, then use S = −I + ssᵀ + εP, with s = √q and a sufficiently small positive ε. Its slow space is exactly the chosen complement. Small ε preserves positive transition rates because the unperturbed reset has strictly positive rates. This construction establishes the bound in the unrestricted reversible family; it does not promise the triplet-failure premise.

4. The full triplet premise is much more restrictive

If n ≥ 4 and only one nonstationary mode is faster than all the others, the full F-and-G conditions force the generator to be a reset plus mixing on one adjacent pair. The fast vector is supported only on that pair. The proof uses the last nonzero component to order the earlier components with G, then F forces all but its adjacent predecessor to vanish.

For four states this covers all repeated-slow cases, and stochastic order gives normal convex-divergence ordering at every time. For five or more states, smaller repeated slow spaces can have multiple faster modes; the same classification cannot be assumed. An exact five-state example has a negative adjacent-difference matrix entry despite all F and G being nonpositive.

A finite-time crossing can disappear again: solve the three-exponential test

1. Subtract the two squared distances

In the symmetric coordinates, different eigenspaces are perpendicular. Squaring the length therefore gives no cross terms. Group repeated eigenvalues together and subtract the nearer preparation from the farther one:

Δ(t) = Σⱼ Aⱼ e2μⱼt,
Aⱼ = ‖Pⱼzfar‖² − ‖Pⱼznear‖².

There are at most three distinct nonzero eigenvalues in four states. A coefficient Aⱼ can vanish even when both individual projections are nonzero; discard that exact zero before identifying the dominant remaining exponential. For two different Gibbs preparations on the same bath side, Δ(0) is strictly positive.

2. Factor out a positive exponential

With three remaining terms the sign is the sign of F(t) = A + Be−at + Ce−bt, where 0 < a < b. Differentiating gives F′ = −aBe−at − bCe−bt. A positive stationary time exists only when BC < 0 and −bC/(aB) > 1. If it exists, it is

t* = ln[−bC/(aB)] / (b−a).

Check F(0), the limiting constant A, and F(t*) when that time is admissible. A negative minimum proves a reversal. A zero minimum does not: the graph may only touch zero or approach it forever. With two terms, the factored expression is monotone, so only endpoints are needed.

3. A hand-solvable illustration

Let x = e−t and F = 1−6x+6x². At t = 0, x = 1 and F = 1. At very late times x tends to zero and F tends to 1 again. But at x = 1/2, F = −1/2. Solving the quadratic gives x = (3 ± √3)/6. Both roots lie strictly between zero and one, producing two finite crossings and a reversed interval between them.

This little polynomial illustrates the sign pattern; it is not by itself a physical generator. The report’s separate exact construction supplies positive reversible rates, genuine thermal preparations, and rational spectral coefficients with precisely this pattern. Its crossing distances are extremely small, so it demonstrates a mathematical possibility rather than an engineering benefit.

See the finite-time theorem and its exact transient construction. The test concerns weighted Euclidean distance and its square; KL and total variation require their own finite-time analysis.

Can a proposed slow pattern come from allowed rates?

1. An attractive curve is not yet a realizable model

You can write down values w₁,…,w₄ that produce an interesting thermal overlap. To realize the pattern, it must be an eigenfunction of a probability-conserving reversible matrix, be its simple slowest nonstationary mode, and satisfy all the rate constraints. The eigenvalue equation alone does not guarantee the last two requirements.

2. Design the two faster directions first

Subtract the bath mean from w, so qᵀw = 0. Choose two independent columns U spanning the directions perpendicular to both the constant function and w in the bath-weighted inner product. For a symmetric two-by-two matrix B, form the signed auxiliary matrix M₀ = −DUBUᵀ. It kills the stationary and proposed slow directions. If B is positive definite, its other two directions have strictly negative eigenvalues.

This auxiliary matrix may have negative off-diagonal entries. Add a sufficiently large reset ρ(q1ᵀ−I). That makes all transition rates positive and shifts all nonstationary eigenvalues by −ρ. The proposed w now has eigenvalue −ρ, strictly slower than the other two. Reset addition leaves every F and G unchanged.

3. The remaining problem is a disk and straight lines

Positive rescaling lets us set trace B = 1:

B = ½ [[1+x, y], [y, 1−x]].
Its eigenvalues are (1 ± √(x²+y²))/2.
Both are positive exactly when x²+y² < 1.

The eight F-and-G inequalities are linear in x and y: each cuts out a closed half-plane. Find their common point closest to the origin. It must be the origin, a perpendicular projection onto one boundary line, or an intersection of two nonparallel boundary lines. These are finitely many candidates. With rational q and w, their coordinates and squared lengths are rational.

A feasible candidate with squared length strictly below one realizes the mode. Equality to one does not: one fast gap vanishes and the proposed slow mode is no longer simple. A test that rounds 1 to 0.999999 would change the mathematical problem. The regression suite includes exact boundary rejection.

4. Necessary conditions need not be sufficient

The inequalities w₁ ≥ w₂, w₁+w₂ ≥ 2w₃, and w₄ ≤ 0 are necessary under the full premise. They do not replace the disk test. The report gives a vector satisfying them strictly but having a positive weighted sum of three required inequalities equal to trace B. The inequalities would force trace B ≤ 0, contradicting positive definiteness. This is an explicit impossibility certificate.

Why one endpoint decides cooling under the full triplet premise

1. Keep the premises visible

This shortcut assumes all four original triplet predicates fail and the slow mode is simple. It does not apply to an arbitrary four-state mode. In the contrast orientation the cooling overlap b is negative, and b′ at the bath is strictly positive.

2. Split according to w₃−w₄

If C = w₃−w₄ is nonnegative, the conditional first-three mean is increasing and stays above w₄. Differentiating b = Pm+(1−P)w₄ gives b′ = Pm′+P′(m−w₄) > 0, so there is no cooling anomaly.

If C < 0, use the derivative curve from the preceding explanation: b′ = gΦ with g > 0 and Φ = A+Bu+Cv(u). Because v(u) is strictly convex, Φ is strictly concave. At the low-temperature endpoint its limit is w₁−w₂ ≥ 0. At the bath it is positive. If its infinite-temperature-end value is also nonnegative, the whole interior lies above the nonnegative chord and has positive derivative.

If that last endpoint value is negative, concavity permits exactly one crossing before reaching the bath. Thus b′ is negative first, then positive forever. Since b is negative on cooling, its absolute value first grows and then shrinks. This gives a unique maximum and finite hot preparations with reversed ordering.

Cooling exists ⇔ b′(0) < 0 ⇔ Covuniform(w,E) > 0.

Here β = 0 is an analytic endpoint used to decide the sign. It is not presented as a finite-temperature witness. Strict negativity persists at nearby positive β. Endpoint equality instead gives strict normal ordering at every finite positive β.

3. Avoid choosing an eigenvector by using a projector

The report computes the same sign using the largest nonzero cubic root r and a scalar quadratic ℛ(r) built from M, M², and the bath probabilities. The key identity is

ℛ(r) / p′(r) = b(0)b′(0) / (wᵀDw).

The denominator on the right is positive, p′(r) > 0, and b(0) < 0. Therefore ℛ(r) > 0 means cooling. The polynomial projector is used only on zero-total displacements; it need not annihilate the stationary direction on the full space. If r is repeated, the separate repeated-mode theorem applies instead of dividing by p′(r) = 0.

The endpoint proof and rate evaluation show each identity and sign.

How exact algorithms decide signs and zeros without a numerical tolerance

1. Turn commensurate energies into polynomials

If Eᵢ = mᵢΔ with integer mᵢ, set x = e−βΔ. Thermal weights become xmᵢ. After shifting all exponents equally, the partition function Z and slow numerator N are polynomials. The derivative sign is the sign of N′Z−NZ′ for a simple mode, or N′Z−2NZ′ for the squared projection amplitude.

Factor this polynomial by multiplicity. A root of odd multiplicity changes sign; a root of even multiplicity merely touches zero. Count the odd roots on the open cooling and heating intervals. Count all distinct numerator roots separately for strong preparations. Remove the bath and the zero/infinite-temperature endpoint factors exactly.

2. Algebraic coefficients need the correct real root

A cubic can define several real numbers. Saying “α is a root” is insufficient: an isolating rational interval selects which one. A polynomial expression in α may have a negative value even if its leading coefficient is positive. For example, the real root α of α³−α−1 = 0 lies between 1 and 2, so α−2 is negative.

The implementation first reduces expressions exactly to detect zero. For a nonzero expression it evaluates rational intervals and refines the selected root interval until zero is excluded. The Sturm sequence counts roots using these correctly embedded signs. It does not infer signs from the appearance of a coefficient list.

3. Incommensurate rational weights lead to prime logarithms

With arbitrary rational bath weights, the full-premise endpoint scalar is a sum of algebraic coefficients times logarithms of rational numbers. Factor those rationals into primes to write ℛ(r) = Σγₚ ln p. Unique prime factorization makes the prime logarithms independent over rational coefficients. The cited form of Baker’s theorem then makes them linearly independent over algebraic coefficients. Consequently the sum is zero exactly when every γₚ is zero. This uses an established theorem; it does not assert algebraic independence of logarithms.

If some coefficient is nonzero, convergent rational logarithm bounds eventually determine the sign. Range reduction keeps the series arguments small. A resource cap can stop refinement early, but the returned answer must then be “unresolved,” not “no cooling.” The tests exercise both exact equality and small opposite signs that need more than one refinement round.

Read the polynomial reduction and prime-logarithm proof. Their rationality and triplet assumptions are substantive, not implementation conveniences that may be dropped.

A real audit finding: a root near the bath was easy for a reference test to miss

1. The problematic shortcut

A supporting test compared the numerical classifier against signs sampled between rational root-enclosing intervals. It retained a root only when the whole enclosure lay strictly inside the temperature interval. An enclosure touching the bath boundary does not prove that its root lies on the boundary: the root can be slightly inside.

2. Reproduce the failure with a thermal polynomial

For Z = 1+x+x²+x³, choose N = x+(−8/17+ε)x³ with ε = 10−30. Its thermal derivative numerator H = N′Z−NZ′ satisfies

H(1/2) = 17ε/16 > 0,
H(1) = −82/17+6ε < 0.

So an interior sign change exists between x = 1/2 and x = 1. But a finite-width root enclosure begins exactly at 1/2 and extends only slightly above it. The old reference skipped that root and sampled a negative value farther inside, missing the tiny positive interval.

3. Repair the reference, then challenge it

The reference now counts odd-multiplicity roots with exact rational arithmetic after exactly removing endpoint factors. It finds the missing root. Thirteen regression checks cover this example, true endpoint roots, even tangencies, repeated odd roots, roots near zero and one, and algebraic coefficient fields. The main exact classifier already used exact endpoint removal and passes this new example unchanged.

This was a defect in a supporting audit reference, not a refutation of the mathematical theorem or a demonstrated error in the production exact classifier. Recording it matters: a checker needs scrutiny too.
