Slow route: prove the single-turn test using first-year calculus

1. Separate the numerator from its positive denominator

Use energies 0 < E₂ < E₃ < E₄, a bath at β = 1, and mode values w = (1, −p, −r, z), where p > r > −z > 0. Here p and r are positive numbers, while z is negative. This proof applies when E₄ > E₂+E₃ and (1+r)E₃ > (p−r)(E₃−E₂). These are extra hypotheses of this particular mechanism.

N(β) = 1 − p e−βE₂ − r e−βE₃ + z e−βE₄,
Z(β) = 1 + e−βE₂ + e−βE₃ + e−βE₄,   b = N/Z.

Differentiating N gives three positive terms: pE₂e−βE₂, rE₃e−βE₃, and −zE₄e−βE₄. Thus N increases strictly. Bath orthogonality says N(1) = 0. Consequently b is negative before the bath and positive after it. There is no other zero.

2. Turn the quotient rule into a sign problem

Set D = N′Z−NZ′. Since Z is positive, the quotient rule gives b′ = D/Z²; D and b′ have exactly the same sign. Expanding the two products pairs states i and j. Their contribution is (wᵢ−wⱼ)(Eⱼ−Eᵢ)e−β(Eᵢ+Eⱼ). Define six positive constants:

c₁ⱼ = (1−wⱼ)Eⱼ   for j = 2,3,4;
cᵢⱼ = (wⱼ−wᵢ)(Eⱼ−Eᵢ)   for 2 ≤ i < j ≤ 4.

The second group is positive because −p < −r < z. The extra energy inequality above is precisely c₁₃ > c₂₃. Multiply D by the positive factor eβE₄ and regroup:

J(β) = c₁₂eβ(E₄−E₂)
+ eβ(E₄−E₂−E₃)(c₁₃eβE₂−c₂₃)
+ c₁₄ − c₂₄e−βE₂ − c₃₄e−βE₃.

For β ≥ 0, the first term increases. Both factors in the second term are positive and increasing. The constant stays constant. Each of the last two negative terms increases toward zero. Therefore J is strictly increasing. Also J(1) > 0, because N(1) = 0 and N′(1) > 0.

3. Check the endpoint, including equality

J(0) = D₀ = (1−p−r+z)(E₂+E₃+E₄) + 4(pE₂+rE₃−zE₄).

If D₀ < 0, continuity gives a zero between 0 and 1, and strict increase makes that zero unique. Before it, b′ < 0. Since b < 0, the magnitude |b| increases there. Any two distinct positive β values in that interval give finite hot and cold preparations with the anomalous ordering. After the zero, |b| decreases back to zero at the bath.

If D₀ = 0, strict increase gives J(β) > 0 at every β > 0. If D₀ > 0, the same conclusion holds. Both cases exclude a cooling turn. On the heating side β > 1, both b and b′ are positive, giving normal heating order.

4. Keep the physical generator attached to the curve

A desired curve is not enough: it must come from the slowest nonconstant mode of an allowed rate matrix. In the controlled family, r = r₀+ηz, where r₀ = 48959/80000 and η = 1/160. This adjustment keeps the bath mean zero. Two rates are solved from the eigenvector equation while four stay fixed. Exact bounds show that every rate remains positive and every original triplet test fails throughout −0.03 ≤ z ≤ −0.004.

The remaining spectral check uses a baseline matrix. After converting detailed balance into a symmetric matrix, ordinary elimination rewrites its quadratic expression as a sum of four nonzero constants times squares. For the baseline shifted by 50, two constants are negative. An invertible change of variables preserves the maximum number of independent negative directions, so exactly two baseline eigenvalues lie below −50. Increasing the two varying rates only adds nonnegative squared differences to the decay expression. Those two eigenvalues therefore stay below −50. The constructed eigenvalue lies between −20 and −19; the fourth is zero. Thus the constructed mode really is the unique slow nonconstant mode.

Substitution gives D₀ = U−Vz with V > 0. Hence the exact onset is z* = U/V. Its decimal enclosure comes from rational upper and lower bounds for the logarithms of the bath weights. The separate fixed-pair threshold solves bh−bc = 0; it is different because a particular pair can miss a short interval of anomalous ordering.

5. Why the effect initially grows quadratically

Write δ = z−z* and d* = ∂D/∂β at (0,z*). The increasing-J proof gives d* > 0. Near the onset, the leading terms are D ≈ −Vδ+d*β. Solving D = 0 gives βm ≈ Vδ/d*. Because Z(0) = 4, integrate b′ ≈ (−Vδ+d*β)/16 from 0 to βm. The change in b is −V²δ²/(32d*), so its negative magnitude grows by V²δ²/(32d*). Smoothness supplies errors of order δ² in the peak location and δ³ in its height. This approximation explains the onset; the exact sign proof above establishes existence.
