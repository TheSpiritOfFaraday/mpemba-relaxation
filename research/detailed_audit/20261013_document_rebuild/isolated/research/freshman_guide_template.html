Step 01 · The question

Understand the claim before checking it

Imagine two preparations of the same system. One begins hotter than the other. Put both in contact with the same cooler bath and let both evolve under the same rules. Usually the hotter preparation starts farther from the bath's equilibrium. The question is whether it can later be closer.

Here “closer” means a specified mathematical distance between probability distributions. It need not mean a lower thermometer reading. The main example has four possible states. A proposed shortcut inspects every group of three states and tries to rule out unusual cooling. The example shows that this shortcut can miss a collective effect.

  1. Verify that the four-state model is a valid thermalizing model.
  2. Apply exactly the stated three-state tests to all four triplets.
  3. Verify that the hotter preparation begins farther away.
  4. Prove that it eventually becomes closer.

A counterexample is one valid case contradicting a universal statement. It does not say that every system behaves that way. This counterexample concerns a finite-temperature conjecture; it does not refute the separate zero-temperature theorem.

Checkpoint: What would not be enough?

A plot with a crossing, without checked inputs and error bounds, would not establish the full claim. Nor would a passing triplet test on only three of the four triplets.

Step 02 · Probability

Think of four boxes holding one unit in total

The numbers p₁, p₂, p₃, p₄ describe how likely the system is to be in each state. Each lies between zero and one, and together they sum to one. A vector is simply an ordered list. We write the probabilities in a column.

p = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)
0.4 + 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 1

These probabilities can also describe the fractions in a very large collection of identical systems. They do not mean that a single classical system is partly in each state.

If a state is assigned a value wᵢ, its probability-weighted average is Σᵢwᵢpᵢ. The symbol Σ means “add the following terms.” For values (1, −1, 0, 0), the average in the example is 0.4 − 0.3 = 0.1. Negative values of w are allowed: w is an observable, not a probability.

Try it: average (2, 0, −1, 3)

2(0.4) + 0(0.3) − 1(0.2) + 3(0.1) = 0.9. We will use exactly this operation to calculate a slow-mode overlap.

Step 03 · The Gibbs distribution

Higher energy is less likely at equilibrium

Assign an energy Eᵢ to each state. At temperature T, give it an unnormalized weight exp(−βEᵢ), where β = 1/(kBT). We choose units with kB = 1 and bath βb = 1. The exponential exp(x) is eˣ; its inverse is the natural logarithm ln.

Z(β) = exp(−βE₁) + exp(−βE₂) + exp(−βE₃) + exp(−βE₄)
πᵢ(β) = exp(−βEᵢ) / Z(β)

Z is just the normalizing total. The Greek letter π here names the equilibrium probability list; it is not the circle constant. A smaller β means a hotter preparation. At β near zero the probabilities are nearly equal. Cooling increases β and favors low-energy states.

It is convenient to store the bath weights qᵢ = exp(−Eᵢ). Then Eᵢ = −ln(qᵢ), and at any preparation temperature the weights are qᵢβ. In this project the bath remains fixed while the preparation changes.

Checkpoint: Which is hotter, β = 0.001 or β = 0.01?

β = 0.001 is hotter. In bath-temperature units their temperatures are 1000 and 100. Both are hotter than the bath at T = 1. These very hot preparations are part of a mathematical counterexample, not the later moderate-range physical model.

Step 04 · Rates

Probability flows between boxes

A transition rate is a probability per unit time, to first order over a very short interval. Rate 2 does not mean probability 2. Over a short time Δt, it gives a transition probability approximately 2Δt. Multiple transitions make this approximation unsuitable for a large Δt.

Mᵢⱼ is the rate from state j to state i. Think “column gives the starting box, row gives the destination.” The diagonal records loss, so it is negative.

Mⱼⱼ = −Σᵢ≠ⱼ Mᵢⱼ
dpᵢ/dt = Σⱼ Mᵢⱼ pⱼ
Each column of M sums to zero.

A matrix is a table of numbers. Multiplying M by p means taking each row's weighted sum. The equation above is a collection of ordinary differential equations. Column sums of zero make the total derivative of p₁ + p₂ + p₃ + p₄ zero. Positive off-diagonal rates ensure that probability cannot flow out of an empty box.

A complete two-box example
M = [ −2   1 ]
    [  2  −1 ]
dp₁/dt = −2p₁ + p₂
dp₂/dt =  2p₁ − p₂

Add the equations to get zero. The equilibrium is (1/3, 2/3): the outgoing flow 2(1/3) equals the returning flow 1(2/3).

Step 05 · Equilibrium

Check every forward-and-backward pair

Detailed balance means that at the bath distribution, each pair has equal flows in both directions:

Mᵢⱼ qⱼ = Mⱼᵢ qᵢ.

Dividing both sides by Q = q₁ + q₂ + q₃ + q₄ gives equal flows for the actual probabilities. There may still be individual transitions at equilibrium; their average flows cancel.

For i < j, let aᵢⱼ be the downward rate from higher-energy j to lower-energy i. To impose detailed balance, define the upward rate as aⱼᵢ = aᵢⱼ qⱼ/qᵢ. Since qⱼ < qᵢ, going up costs energy and is slower.

Example: q₁ = 1, q₂ = 199/200, a₁₂ = 1
a₂₁ = 1 × (199/200)/1 = 199/200
a₁₂ q₂ = 199/200 = a₂₁ q₁.

Check all six pairs and all four column sums. Together these give Mπb = 0: the bath distribution stays fixed.

Step 07 · Four triplets

Apply the original test without changing it

The four ordered triplets are 123, 124, 134, and 234. For each i < j < k, set A = aᵢₖ, B = aⱼₖ, C = aᵢⱼ, t = qⱼ/qᵢ, and u = qₖ/qⱼ. The letters t and u in this test are weight ratios, not time.

κ = (A+B)/[C(1+t)]
G = At − B
F = A(1+tu/2) − B(1+u/2) − C(1−t)
P = (κ ≥ 1 AND F > 0) OR (κ ≤ 1 AND G > 0)

“P is false” means that this necessary-condition test fails. It is not a claim that every passing triplet necessarily has an effect. Notice that κ comparisons include equality, whereas the F and G comparisons do not.

G = (249/10)(199/200) − 4983/200 = −279/2000
κ = 49.815/1.995 > 1
F = −0.020175251256… < −0.02

Only the κ ≥ 1 branch can pass here. But F is negative, so the triplet test fails. Exact arithmetic gives the following bounds for all four:

Why use original rates?

The conjecture specifies rates from the original system. Eliminating a state and replacing its routes by “effective rates” changes the test. If you form an isolated triplet generator, recompute its diagonal losses; deleting a row and column alone leaks probability.

Step 08 · Logic and algebra

Check the useful shortcut in all three cases

The test fails exactly when both F ≤ 0 and G ≤ 0. This is not true of arbitrary pairs of numbers with arbitrary branches; it follows from a special identity.

H = A+B−C(1+t), d = (1−t)/(1+t), h = 2/(1+t)+u/2
F = hG + dH, with h > 0 and d > 0.
  1. H > 0: κ > 1, so failure means F ≤ 0. Rearranging gives hG = F−dH < 0. Thus G < 0.
  2. H < 0: κ < 1, so failure means G ≤ 0. Then F = hG+dH < 0.
  3. H = 0: κ = 1. Both branches apply, and F = hG. Failure means neither strict sign is positive, so F and G are both nonpositive.

Conversely, if both are nonpositive, neither branch can pass. At fixed q, each F and G is linear in the downward rates, so the allowed rates form an intersection of half-spaces. A half-space is simply one side of a flat boundary. Equalities belong to the allowed set.

Checkpoint: If κ = 1 and F = G = 0, does the test pass?

No. The sign tests require strict positivity. This matters in the limiting family used for the 25% theorem.

Step 09 · Exponential decay

A mode is a pattern that keeps its shape

First recall the calculus equation y′ = λy. Its solution is y(t) = y(0)eλt. If λ is negative, the pattern decays. If λ = −2, it decays more slowly than λ = −10.

For several boxes, a right eigenvector v is a pattern satisfying Mv = λv. Starting with that pattern's deviation from equilibrium, the deviation remains proportional to v and its size changes by eλt. A left eigenvector w satisfies wᵀM = λwᵀ. It measures how much of that mode is present.

w = (1, −759/1000, −48957/80000, −1/250)
λ₂ = −4966671/250000 = −19.866684
Σ qᵢwᵢ = 0

For this model, multiplying out all four columns verifies the left equation exactly. A matching right vector is vᵢ = qᵢwᵢ/N, where N = Σqᵢwᵢ². This makes Σwᵢvᵢ = 1. Choosing a scale for w changes the numerical overlap but not the hot/cold amplitude ratio.

Why does a left eigenvector measure an independently decaying component?

Differentiate its average along a trajectory: d(wᵀp)/dt = wᵀp′ = wᵀMp = λwᵀp. The first equality uses that w is fixed while the system evolves. This is the scalar equation y′ = λy again, so wᵀp(t) = eλtwᵀp(0). Also wᵀMπb = 0 = λwᵀπb; since λ is nonzero, its equilibrium average is zero. This explains both the exponential behavior and why b can be calculated directly from the initial probabilities.

Make sure it is the slow mode

Being an eigenvector is not enough. There are three decaying modes. The characteristic polynomial, whose roots are eigenvalues, factors as x(x−λ₂) times a quadratic. Exact opposite signs at −337 and −336 locate one quadratic root; opposite signs at −60 and −59 locate the other. A quadratic has only two roots, so they are accounted for. Both decay faster than λ₂, which lies between −20 and −19.

Why do opposite signs locate a root?

A polynomial is continuous. To go from negative to positive it must pass through zero. The intervals are disjoint, so these are distinct roots. The new checker reconstructs all polynomial coefficients from determinants rather than trusting decimal eigenvalues.

Step 11 · Distance reversal

Connect a smaller overlap to actual relaxation

Define a nonnegative distance-like quantity by squaring each probability error, dividing by qᵢ, and adding:

Dq(p) = Σ (pᵢ−πb,i)²/qᵢ.

It is zero only at equilibrium. The exact initial values satisfy Dq(cold) < 24 < Dq(hot). Thus the hotter preparation really starts farther away according to this measure.

At long times, the faster patterns disappear relative to the slowest one. In more precise notation, p(t)−πb = b(β)v eλ₂t + faster-decaying terms. Insert this into the squared distance. The leading factor shared by both preparations cancels in their ratio:

Dq(hot, t) / Dq(cold, t) → [b(hot)/b(cold)]² < 1.

A limit strictly below one means that after some finite time the ratio stays below one. Initially it is above one. Continuous evolution therefore has at least one crossing in between. This establishes existence, not the earliest crossing time or uniqueness of the crossing.

Formal support: The Lean development proves the evolution equation, uniqueness of its solution, the limiting ordering, and the initial ordering for this rational witness. Its distance is Dq. It does not certify a numerical crossing time or the KL argument below.
What happens to KL distance?

Relative entropy, or KL divergence, is DKL(p‖π) = Σpᵢ ln(pᵢ/πᵢ). Near equilibrium, the Taylor expansion of ln gives DKL ≈ ½Σ(pᵢ−πᵢ)²/πᵢ. Because πᵢ = qᵢ/Q, this is QDq/2. Here Var(E) = Σπᵢ(Eᵢ−U)² is the variance, with mean energy U = ΣπᵢEᵢ. It is positive when distinct energy levels all have positive probability. The same strict late-time ratio follows analytically. For Gibbs preparations, differentiation gives dDKL/dβ = (β−1)Var(E), negative on the cooling interval 0 < β < 1. So the hotter one also starts farther away in KL. This argument is separate from the Lean distance theorem.

Derive the initial KL ordering instead of memorizing it

  1. Let U(β) = Σπᵢ(β)Eᵢ. Differentiating the normalizing sum gives Z′/Z = −U.
  2. The quotient rule gives πᵢ′ = πᵢ(U−Eᵢ). Hence U′ = U²−ΣπᵢEᵢ² = −Var(E).
  3. Because the bath inverse temperature is one, ln(πᵢ(β)/πᵢ(1)) = (1−β)Eᵢ + ln Z(1)−ln Z(β).
  4. Average that expression: DKL = (1−β)U + ln Z(1)−ln Z(β).
  5. Differentiate. The −U and +U terms cancel, leaving DKL′ = (1−β)U′ = (β−1)Var(E).

For 0 < β < 1 this is strictly negative, so a hotter preparation (smaller β) starts with larger KL distance. This conclusion uses distinct energies and strictly positive Gibbs probabilities.

Step 12 · Interpret the numbers

Amplitude, distance, and elapsed time are different

If the amplitude ratio approached 3/4, its squared-distance ratio would approach 9/16. That would mean 25% amplitude advantage and 43.75% leading squared-distance advantage, not 25% time saving.

First-calculus explanation of the time issue

Suppose D(t) ≈ Cb²e−2γt. Solving D(t) = δ gives t ≈ ln(Cb²/δ)/(2γ). The cold-minus-hot time difference approaches ln(|bc|/|bh|)/γ. It is an additive shift. The percentage of total time depends on δ and tends to zero as the threshold tends to zero in this approximation.

Rescaling every rate by a factor c runs the same probability path c times faster. It changes elapsed times, but not the amplitude ratio.

Step 13 · A mechanism you can see

The fourth state dilutes a negative average

The mode values are roughly (1, −0.759, −0.612, −0.004). The highest-energy state contributes almost zero to the average. At very high temperature it nevertheless holds almost one quarter of the probability. That probability dilutes the contribution from the other states.

Cool slightly: the fourth state loses probability rapidly. The negative middle-state contributions can become more prominent, making b more negative. Cool farther toward the bath: the ground state's positive contribution increases and the average returns to zero. This competition allows a small nonmonotonic part of the overlap curve.

The controlled family varies the fourth mode value z from −0.03 to −0.004, with compensating changes elsewhere to keep a valid slow mode. The existence threshold is approximately z* = −0.0179401112. The fixed hot/cold pair needs z > −0.014800248, so it can miss an effect that another pair would detect.

Why is an endpoint slope enough in this special family?

Usually it is not. The proof writes J(β) = eβE₄Z(β)²b′(β), expands it into exponential terms, and shows J is strictly increasing with J(1) > 0. Thus a negative J(0) gives exactly one interior zero; a nonnegative J(0) gives none. That extra monotonicity proof rules out hidden turns. The rate and spectral checks must also hold throughout the family, not just at the plotted points.

Step 14 · The 25% bound

Separate the difficult mode theorem from easy averaging

The difficult part of the theorem proves that the triplet premise restricts the slow mode. With a suitable sign choice:

w₁ ≥ w₂,   w₁+w₂ ≥ 2w₃,   w₄ ≤ 0,
and b(β) < 0 for 0 < β < 1.

These conditions are conclusions about a simple slow mode; simple means its eigenvalue occurs only once. They are not assumptions one is free to add to the conjecture. Their proof uses the full triplet constraints, a change of coordinates, and an elimination argument. The Lean library checks that structural step. The rest can be followed using weighted averages.

  1. Let P(β) be the total probability in states 1, 2, 3. Each has lower energy than state 4, so 3/4 < P < 1 for finite β > 0. Cooling increases P.
  2. Let m(β) be the conditional average of w within these three states: divide their weighted sum by their total probability.
  3. The first two mode inequalities imply that m cannot decrease as β increases. The expansion in the box below proves this.
  4. The full average is b = Pm + (1−P)w₄.
Check the conditional-average derivative

Write (w₁,w₂,w₃) as a constant vector plus a(1,−1,0)+c(1,1,−2), where a = (w₁−w₂)/2 ≥ 0 and c = (w₁+w₂−2w₃)/6 ≥ 0. Put x = e−βE₂, y = e−βE₃. The average of the first pattern is (1−x)/(1+x+y). Its derivative has numerator 2E₂x + E₃y − (E₃−E₂)xy > 0, since x ≤ 1. The second average is 1−3y/(1+x+y), also increasing because the third state's conditional probability decreases. Nonnegative combinations stay nondecreasing.

Prove the two claims about P

Each of the first three Boltzmann weights is strictly larger than the fourth when β > 0 and the energies are ordered. Therefore the fourth probability is less than 1/4, so P = 1−π₄ > 3/4. It is also less than one, because π₄ is positive. For monotonicity, differentiate π₄: π₄′ = π₄(U−E₄). The mean energy U is strictly below the largest energy E₄, so π₄′ < 0 and P′ > 0. All inequalities are strict for the finite admissible parameters.

For a hot and cold pair, let r = Pc/Ph. Then 1 < r < 4/3. Subtract the two averages:

bc − r bh = Pc(mc−mh) + (1−r)w₄ ≥ 0.

Both terms are nonnegative. Since both b's are negative, this says |bc| ≤ r|bh| < (4/3)|bh|. Therefore |bh|/|bc| > 3/4, and the amplitude advantage is strictly less than 1/4.

Step 15 · Sharpness

“Sharp” means arbitrarily close is possible

A bound can be true but wasteful. To show that 25% is sharp, build valid systems approaching it. The formal family uses a small positive ε:

q = (1, 1−ε/3, 3/5, ε²)
w = (1, −4/5, −1/3−4ε/9+5ε³/3, −ε).

Choose ε = e−L², βh = L−3, βc = L−1, with L large and finite. The highest energy is 2L². Its hot weight is e−2/L, approaching one, while its cold weight is e−2L, approaching zero. The first three weights approach one in both preparations.

1 − 4/5 − 1/3 = −2/15
bh → (−2/15)/4 = −1/30
bc → (−2/15)/3 = −2/45
1 − |bh|/|bc| → 1 − 3/4 = 1/4.

This limiting arithmetic alone is not enough: the family must have positive rates, the required triplet signs, and a simple slow mode for sufficiently small positive ε. Those conditions are part of the formal proof. Some triplet expressions are exactly zero, which is permitted by the original strict tests. A different, separately certified family makes all the inequalities strict and retains the same limit.

No finite admissible member attains 25%. The limiting endpoint has singular parameters and is not a valid substitute for a finite witness. Large advantages here require large energy separation.
Step 16 · A different research question

Finite-time gains in a shared-barrier model

The later physical-model study asks for visible finite-time improvement at more moderate parameter values. It uses energies (0, g₁, g₁+g₂, g₁+g₂+g₃), two basins {1,4} and {2,3}, and three shared barrier excesses. A basin here is a group of configurations with relatively fast internal rearrangement.

Bᵢⱼ = max(Eᵢ,Eⱼ) + hᵢⱼ
Mᵢⱼ = ν₀ exp[−(Bᵢⱼ−Eⱼ)]
h₁₄ = hA, h₂₃ = hB, other hᵢⱼ = hX.

B is symmetric between a pair, so Mᵢⱼe−Eⱼ = ν₀e−Bᵢⱼ = Mⱼᵢe−Eᵢ. This verifies detailed balance directly. The attempt frequency ν₀ sets the clock.

The nominal temperatures are about 3.8694 and 9.4721 times the bath temperature. The hotter preparation has less of the slow mismatch between basin populations, despite its larger initial KL distance. The reported continuous certificate establishes:

  • At least 10% KL-distance advantage for every 0.4 ≤ t/τ ≤ 0.8, with cold KL at least 0.001.
  • At least 5% time saving for every threshold 0.001 ≤ δ ≤ 0.01.
  • Both conclusions throughout the specified simultaneous ±5% uncertainty box.

Here τ is the slow relaxation time of the perturbed generator itself. The uncertainty coordinates are the three energy gaps, three barrier excesses, and the two temperature excesses T/Tb−1. They are not ±5% changes in eight arbitrary matrix entries.

Two triplets pass in this model. It is not another all-triplet-failure counterexample. Its exact stochastic bath realization is an idealized model; it does not establish experimental cooling, a material's rate law, or a net benefit after preparation costs.
Step 17 · Continuous verification

Checking many points does not check every point

There are infinitely many points in a box of possible parameters. Even checking all corners can miss an interior failure. Interval arithmetic replaces a number with a lower and upper bound and carries safe bounds through each operation. If a full small box proves the required sign, every point in it passes. If it cannot, split the box and try again.

Example: x ∈ [1,2], y ∈ [3,4] ⇒ x+y ∈ [4,6].
But x−x may be enclosed as [−1,1], even though its actual value is 0.

The second line shows dependency overestimation. An interval engine may fail to prove a true claim because its enclosure is too wide. An unresolved box is neither a proof nor a physical counterexample. Outward rounding, bounds on truncated series, and a complete subdivision tree all matter.

Why eight proof obligations?

  1. Prove the distance advantage throughout its time window.
  2. Prove both preparations are above the threshold band early.
  3. Prove the cold one is below the band late.
  4. Prove cold KL is concave as a function of cold temperature over the needed domain.
  5. Prove hot KL is nonincreasing in hot temperature at threshold crossings.
  6. Prove the hot one is below the band late.
  7. Prove the time comparison at the low-cold/low-hot temperature endpoints.
  8. Prove it at the high-cold/low-hot endpoints.
Follow the temperature reduction with freshman calculus

At a fixed time, a concave curve lies above the chord connecting its endpoints. Let m be the earlier of the two cold endpoint threshold times. At time m, both endpoints are at or above the threshold, so every interior cold temperature is too. No interior cold temperature arrives earlier than m.

For the hot trajectory, differentiate D(T,u(T)) = δ: u′(T) = −DT/Du. The certificate establishes DT ≤ 0 at these roots, and relaxation gives Du < 0. Hence u′ ≤ 0: the lowest hot temperature is slowest. Verifying both cold endpoints against this worst hot endpoint therefore covers all preparation temperatures. The remaining six physical parameters still need continuous coverage.

A successful replay must end with no pending cells and no enclosure errors. The replay alone does not prove that every formula in the implementation is correct: this review also needs the derivations, rounding behavior, input correspondence, independent calculations, and negative controls.

Near-bath KL windowSmall enough thermal perturbations satisfy a normal finite-time order under the stated G premise.The sufficient window uses |β−βb|(En−E₁) ≤ 2/√5; no claim that this constant is optimal.
More than four statesConstruct examples approaching amplitude advantage 1−3/n.The general upper bound 1−2/n leaves a gap for n > 4. Sharpness is established here only for n = 4.
ObstructionsA counterexample breaks an attempted method of proof.Breaking a proof method does not necessarily refute the target theorem or produce a thermal effect.
Activated intermediatesAdd short-lived configurations to examine the physical clock and coarse graining.The expanded model lies outside the original four-state parameter box; sampled behavior is not a continuous certificate.
