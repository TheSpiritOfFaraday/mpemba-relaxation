Why an ordered chain of contrasts preserves every convex heating distance

1. Start with an average that cannot be negative

If two quantities increase together, their covariance is nonnegative. For values aᵢ and an increasing function g, with positive weights pᵢ summing to one, expand the two sides to check

Cov(a,g(a)) = ½ ΣᵢΣⱼ pᵢpⱼ(aᵢ−aⱼ)(g(aᵢ)−g(aⱼ)) ≥ 0.

Each product is nonnegative because its two factors have the same sign. For a convex differentiable function Φ, the slope Φ′ is increasing, so we can use g = Φ′. Strict convexity makes the covariance positive whenever at least two a values differ.

2. Apply that fact to a prefix of physical states

Write the density ratio rᵢ = pᵢ/qᵢ as r = 1+Hx, where H contains the unnormalized prefix contrasts. On heating, all x coordinates are positive. Put gₖ = ΣᵢqᵢHᵢₖΦ′(rᵢ); this is the derivative of the divergence with respect to coordinate xₖ. Individual gₖ need not be positive. Instead look at cumulative sums Sₖ = Σⱼ≤ₖ xⱼgⱼ.

Higher contrasts are constant on states 1 through k+1. Their combined contribution there is cₖ = 1+Σⱼ>ₖxⱼ. The lower contrasts have zero weighted mean on that prefix. Therefore

Sₖ = Σᵢ≤ₖ₊₁ qᵢ(rᵢ−cₖ)Φ′(rᵢ) ≥ 0.

This is exactly prefix mass times the covariance just derived. Notice the distinction: cumulative sums are positive even when individual terms can be negative.

3. Learn the small summation trick that uses those prefixes

Let y = ∂x/∂β and Rₖ = yₖ/xₖ. If R₁ ≥ R₂ ≥ … > 0, then collecting the coefficient of each xₖgₖ gives

ΣₖRₖxₖgₖ = Rn−1Sn−1 + Σₖ<ₙ₋₁(Rₖ−Rₖ₊₁)Sₖ ≥ 0.

This is summation by parts. For three terms, expand R₃S₃+(R₂−R₃)S₂+(R₁−R₂)S₁ to recover the original sum. The identity turns information about partial sums into information about the weighted total.

4. Explain why the ratios keep their order

Initially each xₖ is proportional to δ times an integral of Q(s)e−δs, truncated at Eₖ₊₁ and divided by the relative partition function. Here δ = β−βb > 0 and Q(s) is the bath mass below energy s. Differentiating its logarithm gives a common term minus the mean s in that truncated integral. Extending the upper endpoint increases that mean, so Rₖ decreases with k.

A tridiagonal Metzler contrast generator only couples neighboring contrast coordinates, with nonnegative off-diagonal entries. Its exponential has nonnegative ordered 2×2 determinants. The determinant expansion then preserves decreasing yₖ/xₖ ratios. One can prove the determinant claim by differentiating those determinants: their evolution again has nonnegative off-diagonal coefficients because a neighboring index cannot cross another occupied index.

This condition concerns contrast coordinates, not neighboring physical energy states. It is also a real restriction: a general G-admissible generator can destroy the ratio order. The resulting proof-route failure is not itself a KL reversal.

5. Keep track of strictness and nonsmooth distances

At finite time a distinct initial state cannot become exactly equilibrium, because a matrix exponential is invertible. Thus the full covariance is positive for strictly convex Φ, and the last R is positive. Integrating the positive thermal derivative proves strict heating order. For nonsmooth convex functions, ordered subgradients replace Φ′ almost everywhere; local Lipschitz continuity permits integration. Non-strict convexity gives the non-strict conclusion.

See the full argument and the classical determinant result in Schwarz (1970), Theorem 2. The argument above explains the project’s application; the determinant theorem is established mathematics.

A guaranteed KL window: turn temperature distance into an angle estimate

1. Form a dimensionless measure of how far you start from the bath

Let δ = |β−βb| and c = Emax−Emin. The product z = δc is dimensionless: inverse temperature times energy. Small z means the Gibbs weights have not spread very far relative to the bath. The result guarantees ordinary KL ordering on either side whenever z ≤ 2/√5, approximately 0.8944. Cooling still requires positive initial β.

2. Compare a vector with the direction in which it is growing

After choosing the outward sign, write the positive contrast coordinates as x and their outward thermal derivative as y. Integral formulas for the initial Gibbs weights give, coordinate by coordinate,

(1/δ−c)x ≤ y ≤ (1/δ+c)x.

The nonnegative contrast semigroup preserves both inequalities at every time. Thus y points broadly in the same direction as x. More precisely, if a = 1/δ−c and b = 1/δ+c, the cosine of their angle is at least 2√(ab)/(a+b) = √(1−z²), provided z < 1.

To derive this without guessing an angle, put vᵢ = yᵢ/xᵢ and assign weight xᵢ²/‖x‖². Since a ≤ vᵢ ≤ b, the inequality (vᵢ−a)(vᵢ−b) ≤ 0 gives E[v²] ≤ (a+b)E[v]−ab. The squared cosine is E[v]²/E[v²]. Its lower bound reduces to the nonnegative square ((a+b)E[v]−2ab)².

3. A positive matrix can change the angle, but only by a bounded amount

KL is curved, rather than exactly a squared Euclidean distance. Its Hessian describes that curvature. Along the straight line from equilibrium to the evolved density r, the integrated Hessian T has all eigenvalues between m = 1/rmax and M = 1/rmin. The initial density range has rmax/rmin = ez, and Markov averaging keeps the evolved density inside that range.

The outward KL derivative is yᵀTx. Split T into its scalar center (M+m)I/2 and a remainder of norm at most (M−m)/2. The scalar part rewards alignment; the remainder can reduce the result only by its norm times ‖x‖‖y‖. Hence

∂DKL/∂δ ≥ ½(M+m)[√(1−z²)−tanh(z/2)]‖x‖‖y‖.

4. Check the advertised endpoint, rather than assuming a small-parameter approximation

For 0 < z ≤ 2/√5, squaring the nonnegative sides gives √(1−z²) ≥ z/2. Also tanh(u) < u for positive u, so the bracket is strictly positive even at the upper endpoint. Both vectors remain nonzero at every finite time. The bath endpoint itself has zero KL; it does not require substituting δ = 0 into 1/δ.

This is an exact sufficient bound, uniform in time, rather than a truncated Taylor expansion. Repeated eigenvalues do not invalidate it. Beyond this window the proof becomes inconclusive; it does not predict a reversal. The numerical audit supports the proof on sampled cases, but its finite samples cannot establish the universal theorem.

What is proved about higher dimensions, and what remains a conjecture?

1. Combine contributions only after checking their signs

The contrast theorem writes the oriented slow mode as a nonnegative combination of prefix contrasts. Group its first two contrasts into f, supported on the first three states. If the additional inequality w₁+w₂ ≥ 2w₃ holds, f has a conditional thermal average that strictly increases toward its bath value zero. On cooling that average is negative.

Its full overlap is the conditional mean times the probability P₃ of being in the first three states. At finite positive β, 3/n < P₃ < 1. Therefore its cold-to-hot magnitude ratio is less than n/3: the conditional magnitude shrinks and the probability factor grows by less than n/3.

Each later contrast similarly has a ratio less than n/(k+1), hence at most the stronger threshold n/4. All nonzero components are negative on cooling, so no cancellation occurs. The ratio of their sum is a weighted average of their ratios, with hot magnitudes as positive weights. It is consequently below n/3.

Additional arithmetic inequality ⇒ amplitude advantage < 1−3/n.
For n > 4, the full triplet premise has not yet been proved to imply that additional inequality.

2. One special tail can be compressed exactly for this purpose

Suppose the slow mode takes one constant value on states 4 through n. Let QT be their total bath probability, and require QT < q₃. Average the rates out of this tail using its conditional bath probabilities, and sum the reverse rates. This gives a four-state reversible compression with ordered weights (q₁,q₂,q₃,QT).

The constant-tail eigenfunction survives the compression. The variational bound on eigenvalues keeps it simple and slow. Direct rate identities preserve every F and G inequality: the new F is an average of old F values plus nonnegative multiples of old nonpositive G values. The established four-state theorem then supplies the missing arithmetic inequality.

Full dynamic lumpability is unnecessary because only this eigenfunction is being retained. But a constant tail and a sufficiently small total tail weight are essential assumptions. This averaging is different from eliminating a state through a Schur complement.

3. Convert a proposed mode into a graph construction

Fix positive contrast coordinates x. Connect contrast coordinates i and j with symmetric nonnegative weights ℓᵢⱼ. Choose the diagonal of B to make Bx = 0. The central identity is

−yᵀηBy = Σᵢ<ⱼ ℓᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ(yᵢ/xᵢ−yⱼ/xⱼ)² ≥ 0.

Here η records the squared lengths of the unnormalized contrast columns. The right side resembles a network’s energy: it vanishes exactly when yᵢ/xᵢ is constant on each connected component. For a connected graph, the kernel therefore has only the one direction x. All other directions decay.

Transform B back to physical coordinates. Some rates may initially be negative. Adding a sufficiently large reset makes them positive, shifts every nonstationary eigenvalue equally, and leaves every F and G test unchanged. For fixed bath weights and x, the triplet tests are linear in ℓ, so a linear program can ask whether all graph edges and test margins can be strictly positive together.

4. Understand the exact scope of this decision reduction

A positive optimum realizes the prescribed mode under strict tests. A failed strict program does not reject every fixed mode on a weak boundary: there connectivity, rather than positivity of every edge, is the right condition. A global strict violation of the arithmetic inequality could be perturbed into the strict case and represented by rational construction data.

This turns the open question into a precise feasibility problem. It does not solve that problem for all inputs. Nor would an arithmetic counterexample alone establish a larger thermal advantage; actual Gibbs overlaps would still have to violate the proposed bound.
