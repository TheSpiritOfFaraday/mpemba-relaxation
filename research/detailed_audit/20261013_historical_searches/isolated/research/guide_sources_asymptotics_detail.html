Why approaching 25% can require enormous energy separation

1. A limit does not tell you how quickly you get there

The sequence 1−1/n and the sequence 1−1/log(n) both approach 1 as n grows, but the second approaches much more slowly. In the report, the amplitude advantage approaches 25%. A separate calculation is needed to describe the remaining gap.

2. Replace a tiny parameter with a large, readable one

The family uses a small positive number ε. Set L = log(1/ε), so ε = exp(−L). Its highest energy is 2L in bath-scaled units. Increasing L means increasing the energy separation. Define a = log(5/3), y = exp(−aβ), and x = exp(−2Lβ). The third-state weight y changes slowly with inverse temperature β; the highest-state weight x changes quickly.

Leading positive slow amplitude:
R(β) = (y/3 − 1/5)/(2 + y + x).

The exact formula differs by order ε on the cooling interval. “Order ε” means bounded by a constant times ε for sufficiently small ε, not equal to ε. Differentiation can introduce the large factor 2L, so the derivative error must be checked separately; it is order Lε.

3. Locate the balance between two competing changes

Emptying the highest state reduces the denominator and initially increases the amplitude. Changing the third-state weight eventually reduces the numerator enough to reverse that trend. Differentiate the quotient, using y′ = −ay and x′ = −2Lx. After multiplying by the positive denominator factor, the leading stationary-point equation is:

x[(10L−5a)y−6L] − 13ay = 0.

Near the maximum, y approaches 1. The bracket therefore behaves like 4L, giving x approximately 13a/(4L). Since x = exp(−2Lβ), taking logarithms gives the first approximation to the maximizing inverse temperature:

βₘ ≈ log(4L/(13a))/(2L).

The report also proves uniqueness using its earlier bound on stationary points. A numerical root solver by itself cannot establish that there are no other maxima.

4. Control the error in stages

The initial equation still contains an error proportional to βₘ. First absorb that term into the left side to prove βₘ is at most a constant times log(L)/L. Only then substitute the new bound back to obtain the smaller error of order log(L)/L². This two-stage argument prevents using an unproved estimate to justify itself.

Advantage ≈ 1/4 − [13a/(16L)]·[log(4L/(13a)) + 1].

The remaining deficit is of order log(L)/L. Because the highest energy is 2L, this explains why a clear separation between slow and fast decay modes does not guarantee a near-25% advantage at modest energy separation.
