How a computer proves a whole parameter box instead of sampling it

1. Replace a guessed number with an enclosure

A floating-point calculation returns a nearby representable number. An interval calculation returns two numbers guaranteed to lie below and above the exact answer. For example, if x ∈ [2,3] and y ∈ [4,5], then x+y ∈ [6,8] and xy ∈ [8,15]. The guarantee concerns every allowed pair, including pairs you never sampled.

Computer endpoints themselves are rounded. The implementation widens each basic arithmetic result to the neighboring representable value in the outward direction. It uses MPFR directed rounding for decimal inputs, exponentials, logarithms, and square roots. These guarantees rely on the stated arithmetic environment and compilation settings; the compiler is instructed not to use fast-math or fused contraction.

A denominator interval containing zero does not justify division. A logarithm interval reaching zero does not justify taking a logarithm. Those cases become unresolved calculations that need narrower cells. They never become successful certificates by ignoring the invalid part.

2. Understand why a valid enclosure can still be too wide

If x ∈ [0,1], ordinary interval subtraction gives x−x ∈ [−1,1], although the true result is exactly zero. The enclosure is valid but loses the fact that both appearances represent the same x. This dependency problem can make a correct method fail to certify a true inequality.

The physical checker reduces this overestimation in several ways. It transforms constant edge matrices before multiplying uncertain rates, preserving useful cancellations. It also computes a value at the box center and adds derivative bounds times coordinate displacements:

f(box) ⊆ f(center) + Σₖ [∂f/∂xₖ](box)·(xₖ−centerₖ).

This is the multivariable mean-value bound. Intersecting it with a direct interval evaluation is safe when both enclose the same function. A narrower intersection is stronger evidence; an empty intersection is an error or unresolved calculation, never proof of the desired claim.

3. Reduce the dynamics without discarding a state

Probability deviations sum to zero, so the fourth deviation equals minus the first three. This leaves three independent coordinates. A fixed invertible basis changes how those coordinates are represented; it does not change the dynamics. The basis is near a numerical eigenbasis, but the proof does not assume its entries are exact eigenvectors. Its decimal entries and inverse are enclosed directly.

The generator’s slow eigenvalue is found inside a bracket where the characteristic polynomial has opposite endpoint signs and a positive derivative. Interval Newton steps narrow the bracket while retaining every possible root. A separate bound proves that both other decay rates are faster. This justifies using the slow relaxation time τ = −1/λ, including how that clock changes with the uncertain physical parameters.

4. Bound the matrix exponential and its truncation error

The slow part evolves exactly as e−u times its spectral projector, where u = t/τ. The remaining two-dimensional block is enclosed by splitting it into a diagonal part and an off-diagonal part. The first retained terms can be evaluated explicitly. The omitted terms are bounded by a convergent exponential-series tail; their parameter derivatives receive their own tail bounds.

A second formula expresses the same fast-block exponential using hyperbolic functions. It evaluates the entire functions cosh(√z) and sinh(√z)/√z through formulas that remain finite at z = 0. This avoids dividing by a fast eigenvalue gap that might vanish. For the relevant obligations, the checker intersects this enclosure with the truncated-series enclosure.

“The neglected terms look small” would be insufficient. The actual proof adds an explicit bound for all neglected terms. Tests near zero and on both sides of the implementation’s series/formula boundary check that the two evaluation branches behave consistently.

5. Accept cells only when the complete inequality follows

For the distance benchmark, each accepted cell must establish both 0.9Dcold−Dhot ≥ 0 and Dcold ≥ 0.001. The second condition prevents an apparently impressive relative advantage from being certified solely at a distance below the declared floor.

If an interval sign is unresolved, divide a coordinate interval at a shared midpoint. The two closed children cover their parent exactly. Repeat until each leaf is accepted. A heuristic may choose the split coordinate, but the heuristic never supplies the sign proof.

Success requires an empty queue. A node limit with pending cells means incomplete coverage. It does not prove that the physical claim is false, and it cannot be presented as a complete certificate. A deliberate zero-node-budget test confirms that this implementation returns an incomplete result with one pending cell.

6. Keep each layer of evidence in its proper role

The independent checker uses the original four-state matrix and 70-digit direct exponentials, avoiding the reduced spectral implementation. Its 6,744 value comparisons and 360 derivative comparisons passed. The primitive boundary suite adds 295 comparisons and invalid-domain checks. Equal-preparation controls correctly reject false distance and time-saving claims.

Those checks challenge implementation errors. The continuous conclusion additionally needs the analytic enclosure argument and completed whole-domain coverage. The 54-step review records that chain of reasoning.

Why eight proof obligations cover all temperatures, times, and thresholds

1. Specify what the uncertainty actually changes

The box perturbs eight independent coordinates: three energy gaps, three shared barrier excesses, and two temperature excesses T/Tbath−1. Each ranges over ±5% of its nominal value. The rates are rebuilt from these quantities, preserving detailed balance. This is a different domain from independently changing eight matrix entries or changing the full temperatures by ±5%.

2. Put every threshold crossing inside one common time interval

The threshold band is 0.001 ≤ δ ≤ 0.01. Three endpoint obligations establish that both preparations have KL distance above 0.01 at u = 0.1 and below 0.001 at u = 2. KL decreases strictly along each nonequilibrium trajectory of this positive reversible model, so each threshold has exactly one crossing in that time interval.

This monotonicity is a mathematical property of the trajectory, not an inference from a plot. In density ratios rᵢ = pᵢ/qᵢ, the time derivative is a negative sum of products (rᵢ−rⱼ)(ln rᵢ−ln rⱼ). Every product is nonnegative because logarithm is increasing; positivity of all transition rates gives strictness away from equilibrium.

3. Show that the earliest cold crossing is controlled by an endpoint temperature

A continuous derivative obligation proves that cold KL is concave as a function of its preparation temperature for all relevant physical parameters and times. A concave graph lies above the chord connecting its endpoints.

Fix a threshold and take the earlier of the two cold endpoint crossing times. At that time both endpoint distances are at least the threshold. The chord is therefore at least the threshold, and so is every interior cold temperature’s distance. Every interior cold crossing must occur at least as late as that earlier endpoint crossing.

4. Show which hot temperature is worst

Another obligation proves ∂Dhot/∂T ≤ 0 whenever its distance is in the threshold band. At the crossing, implicit differentiation gives

∂uhot/∂T = −(∂Dhot/∂T)/(∂Dhot/∂u) ≤ 0.

The denominator is negative. Thus increasing the hot preparation temperature cannot delay its threshold crossing in the certified region. The lowest allowed hot temperature is the worst case for this comparison.

5. Prove two boundary-temperature implications

It remains to compare that lowest hot temperature against each cold endpoint. Two obligations prove, throughout the six-dimensional physical box and the relevant time interval,

0.001 ≤ Dcold(u) ≤ 0.01
⇒ Dhot(0.95u) ≤ Dcold(u).

At a cold threshold crossing, this means the hot trajectory has reached the same threshold by at most 95% of the cold time. The preceding temperature arguments then extend those two comparisons to every allowed pair of preparation temperatures.

6. Use the threshold condition without discarding difficult cells

Let F = Dcold(u)−Dhot(0.95u). On the threshold band both Dcold−0.01 and 0.001−Dcold are nonpositive. Therefore, for any chosen α ≥ 0, either of these whole-cell inequalities is sufficient:

F+α(Dcold−0.01) ≥ 0,
F+α(0.001−Dcold) ≥ 0.

The multiplier can reduce interval overestimation. Its proposed value is a heuristic, but acceptance requires a rigorous bound on the resulting expression. A cell may be excluded only when its entire distance enclosure lies outside the band. Moving a minimum to a face does not permit excluding the original cell merely because that face lies outside the band.

7. Count the obligations and bind them to the exact inputs

The eight obligations are: one distance window; both early endpoints; cold late endpoint; cold temperature concavity; hot temperature monotonicity on the band; hot late endpoint; and two boundary-temperature time comparisons. All are needed for the stated combined conclusion.
