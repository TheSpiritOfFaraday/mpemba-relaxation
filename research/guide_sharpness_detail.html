Slow route: prove that valid systems really approach 25%

1. Keep two different constructions separate

Both constructions use the q and w printed above, but their rates differ. The simpler family is formalized in Lean and proves that 25% is the supremum. A second family proves the stronger claim that the slow mode can stay uniformly separated from faster modes. A small perturbation of that second family makes all eight F and G inequalities strict. The stronger gap and perturbation claims are checked analytically and by exact arithmetic, outside the Lean theorem.

2. A continuity argument gives actual positive parameters

For the simpler family, set rate a₁₂ = 1 and define the other rates by the rational functions in the formal sharpness appendix. A rational function is a polynomial divided by a polynomial. If its denominator is nonzero at ε = 0, its value changes continuously near zero. If its value there is positive, it remains positive on some interval around zero.

The proof needs thirteen such functions to be positive simultaneously. They control the three polynomial denominators, five varying rates, three F signs and two G signs. Their values at zero, in the appendix's order, are

68, 12, 21, 3/2, 20/17, 15/7, 20/17, 12/17,
9/14, 117/70, 66/425, 276/595, 1/17.

All are positive. Each has some positive interval where its sign persists. The smallest of finitely many positive interval widths is still positive, so all thirteen conditions hold together for sufficiently small ε > 0. This is an existence proof for an interval; it does not claim that substituting ε = 0 gives a valid bath.

Two triplet expressions, F₁₂₄ and G₂₃₄, are exactly zero. Another, G₁₂₄, tends to zero. Its sign is established by factoring out ε: −G₁₂₄ is ε times the thirteenth positive function. G₁₃₄ < 0 follows from G₂₃₄ = 0 and G₁₂₄ < 0. This accounts for all eight F and G expressions, including the ones continuity alone would not decide.

For small positive ε, the bath weights are positive and strictly decreasing. Exact substitution checks conservation, detailed balance, and Mᵀw = −3w. The five rate limits are positive as well. No numerical eigenvalue calculation is needed.

3. Verify that −3 is the simple slow eigenvalue

The three contrast coordinates of w are positive. The contrast matrix B has nonnegative off-diagonal entries, and the two entries connecting its first coordinate to each of the other two are strictly positive. Here is a useful direct algebraic test for simplicity.

Let A = diag(η)(−3I−B). It is symmetric, Ax = 0 for the positive coordinate vector x, and its off-diagonal entries are nonpositive. Expanding the squares gives, for every y,

yᵀAy = Σᵢ<ⱼ (−Aᵢⱼ)xᵢxⱼ(yᵢ/xᵢ−yⱼ/xⱼ)² ≥ 0.

Thus −3 is at least as large as every contrast eigenvalue. Equality in this sum forces y₁/x₁ = y₂/x₂ and y₁/x₁ = y₃/x₃, because the two connecting coefficients are positive. Hence every zero vector of A is a multiple of x: the eigenvalue is simple. All nonstationary modes lie in this contrast space, so this is the desired slow-mode statement for the full generator.

4. Join admissibility to the thermal limit

The parameter ε = e−L² is positive for each finite L and tends to zero as L grows. It eventually enters the interval where every rate and sign check holds. At the same time, L > 1 gives 0 < L−3 < L−1 < 1, so both preparations are finite and on the cooling side.

The overlap limits above are nonzero and have a strict magnitude difference of 1/90. Therefore sufficiently late members have a strict ordinary cooling reversal. Their advantages approach 1/4. This proves that any proposed upper bound below 1/4 eventually fails, while Step 14 proves every actual advantage remains below 1/4. Those are the two parts of a supremum proof.

5. Why the stronger family has a uniform gap

The second construction chooses two vectors U that are perpendicular, in the bath-weighted dot product, to both the constant vector and w. They span the two faster directions. A symmetric two-by-two matrix B is chosen using the linear equations F₁₂₄ = G₂₃₄ = 0. Here B denotes this new matrix, not the contrast matrix in the previous paragraphs.

M = −diag(q) U(B/ε²)Uᵀ + 3(q1ᵀ−I).

The first term leaves the constant and slow directions alone; the reset term gives w eigenvalue −3. In the faster space the extra decay rates are the two positive eigenvalues ν₁,ν₂ of BJ/ε², where J = Uᵀdiag(q)U. Exact inequalities show B is positive definite: B₁₁ > 0 and det B > 0. The columns of U are independent, so J is positive definite too. Their product has positive real eigenvalues because it is similar to a symmetric positive definite matrix.

For any positive numbers ν₁,ν₂, their product divided by their sum is strictly smaller than either one. The certificate proves det(BJ/ε²)/trace(BJ/ε²) > 1/3. Thus both faster eigenvalues lie below −3−1/3. The same certificate verifies all twelve positive rates and every triplet sign for 0 < ε ≤ 1/30, using thirty exact polynomial sign certificates.

6. Make the triplet inequalities strict without losing the limit

Add ε⁴H, where H is a reversible generator with all eight triplet expressions strictly negative and reduced conductances at most 100. Its largest possible effect on a unit-vector quadratic expression is at most 100ε⁴. The old slow-to-fast gap therefore shrinks by at most 200ε⁴, remaining above 1/4.

Projecting the perturbed slow vector onto the old faster space bounds that component by 100ε⁴/(1/3−100ε⁴). After matching sign and weighted length, the total weighted vector change is less than 500ε⁴. Each bath probability is at least ε²/3, so each ordinary coordinate changes by less than 1000ε³. An average against any probability distribution changes by no more than that largest coordinate error. The error tends to zero, leaving the overlap limits intact. Linearity of F and G makes every formerly zero constraint strictly negative.

The finite example ε = 10−50, βh = 1/10000 and βc = 1/50 has a certified amplitude advantage between 22.6171597478% and 22.6171597506% before perturbation, and above 22% afterward. This is a finite mathematical example with very large energy separation.

A useful restriction when the energy span is limited

Let Δ = E₄−E₁ and d = (βc−βh)Δ. Write p4,h for the hottest preparation's probability in the highest state. Step 14 actually proves |bc|/|bh| ≤ Pc/Ph, where P is the first-three probability mass. Invert these positive ratios to obtain an advantage at most 1−Ph/Pc.

Shift E₁ to zero. The sum Z₃ of the first three unnormalized weights decreases on cooling, while the fourth weight changes by the factor e−d. Consequently π4,c/Pc ≥ e−dp4,h/Ph. Rearranging gives

Advantage ≤ p4,h(1−e−d) < (1−e−d)/4.

To achieve an advantage at least g > 0, it is therefore necessary that d > −ln(1−4g) and p4,h > g. For 10%, 20% and 24%, the required d values are respectively greater than ln(5/3), ln(5), and ln(25). These conditions only rule out impossible targets; satisfying them does not guarantee an effect.
